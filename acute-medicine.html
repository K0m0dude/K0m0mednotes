Acute medicine factfiles

Twenty-five core conditions for the acute ward. Each one covers background and pathophysiology, diagnosis and signs, investigations, treatment, and the emergency protocols to know about.

Study aid, not a prescribing guide. Written for a 4th-year student from standard UK guidance (NICE, BTS/SIGN, Resuscitation Council UK, JBDS, UK Kidney Association and similar). Doses and thresholds are for orientation. Always check the BNF and your trust's guidelines, and never act on them without a senior.

Before your first ward roundRead first

Know before you walk in

  • The one-line story: age, sex, presenting complaint, key background, day of admission, working diagnosis.
  • Obs trend and NEWS2: look at the chart, not just the latest set. Is it going up or down?
  • Fluid balance and weight: urine output, in versus out, IV fluids running, catheter status.
  • Latest bloods against baseline: creatinine, K⁺, Na⁺, CRP, WCC, Hb, lactate, troponin, LFTs, INR as relevant.
  • Drug chart: new antibiotics (day number, indication, stop or review date), held drugs (ACEi, metformin, anticoagulants), VTE prophylaxis, oxygen prescription, allergies.
  • Escalation status: DNACPR or ReSPECT form, treatment escalation plan, capacity issues.
  • Pending: imaging, cultures, referrals, results the team is waiting for.

Presenting a patient

Keep it structured and short: "Mr X is a 72-year-old man, day 3 of admission with … on a background of … Overnight … Today he is [comfortable / unwell], obs are … (NEWS2 …). On examination … Bloods show … My impression is … and the plan is …"

Ground rules

  • Any patient who looks worse than you expected: stay with them, get a nurse to help, call your senior. Don't go and look something up first.
  • Altered consciousness, sweaty, shaky or drowsy: check capillary glucose straight away.
  • Ask "what is the worst thing this could be?" for every patient, then "what would rule it out?"
  • Don't prescribe, give drugs or do procedures without supervision. Do take the history, examine fully, look up the trend and volunteer a differential.
  • Good questions for a senior: "What would make you escalate?", "What are you watching for overnight?", "Why that antibiotic?"
Diabetes mellitus (type 1, type 2), DKA and pancreatic diabetesEndocrine

Background and pathophysiology

  • Type 1 (T1DM): autoimmune destruction of pancreatic β-cells (anti-GAD, IA-2, ZnT8 antibodies) leading to absolute insulin deficiency. Usually younger, lean, rapid onset. Needs insulin for life. Without insulin, unrestrained lipolysis leads to ketogenesis.
  • Type 2 (T2DM): insulin resistance plus progressive relative β-cell failure. Risk factors: obesity, age, family history, South Asian, Black African-Caribbean and Middle Eastern ethnicity, gestational diabetes, PCOS.
  • Pancreatic (type 3c) diabetes: loss of islet cells after chronic pancreatitis, pancreatic cancer or pancreatectomy, cystic fibrosis, haemochromatosis. Both insulin and glucagon are lost, so it is brittle with a high risk of hypoglycaemia. Exocrine failure is common (steatorrhoea, weight loss, needs pancreatic enzyme replacement such as Creon).
  • Other causes: steroids, Cushing's, acromegaly, thiazides, antipsychotics, checkpoint inhibitors (autoimmune diabetes and DKA).
  • DKA: insulin deficiency with counter-regulatory hormone excess leads to hyperglycaemia, ketone production, osmotic diuresis, dehydration and metabolic acidosis. Total body K⁺ is depleted even if the measured K⁺ is normal or high.
  • HHS: severe hyperglycaemia and dehydration with enough insulin to suppress ketosis. Older T2DM patients, developing over days, high mortality.
  • Complications: micro- (retinopathy, nephropathy, neuropathy) and macrovascular (IHD, stroke, PAD). Diabetic foot disease is a major cause of admission.

Diagnosis and clinical signs

Diagnostic criteria: symptoms plus random glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L, or fasting ≥7.0, or HbA1c ≥48 mmol/mol (6.5%). If asymptomatic, confirm with a second test. HbA1c is unreliable with anaemia, haemoglobinopathy, recent transfusion, pancreatic disease and rapid-onset T1DM.
  • Classic symptoms: polyuria, polydipsia, weight loss, fatigue, blurred vision, recurrent infections (thrush, skin).
  • DKA: vomiting, abdominal pain (can mimic an acute abdomen), Kussmaul breathing (deep, rapid), acetone breath, dehydration (dry mucosa, tachycardia, hypotension), drowsiness. Precipitants: infection, missed insulin, new diagnosis, MI, pancreatitis, steroids, SGLT2 inhibitors (euglycaemic DKA, glucose may be under 14).
  • DKA diagnosis (JBDS): all three of glucose >11 mmol/L or known diabetes, ketones ≥3.0 mmol/L (or urine ketones 2+), and venous pH <7.3 or bicarbonate <15 mmol/L.
  • Severe DKA (needs senior or critical care input): ketones >6, pH <7.0, bicarbonate <5, K⁺ <3.5, GCS <12, SBP <90, HR >100 or <60, anion gap >16.
  • HHS: glucose ≥30 mmol/L, osmolality ≥320 mosm/kg, hypovolaemia, ketones <3, pH >7.3 and bicarbonate >15.
  • Hypoglycaemia: glucose <4 mmol/L. Sweating, tremor, palpitations, hunger, confusion, seizure, coma. Ask about sulfonylureas and insulin, missed meals, alcohol, renal failure.

Investigations

  • Capillary glucose and capillary ketones (beta-hydroxybutyrate).
  • Venous blood gas: pH, bicarbonate, K⁺, lactate. Calculate the anion gap: (Na⁺ + K⁺) − (Cl⁻ + HCO₃⁻).
  • U&E (AKI is common), plasma osmolality, FBC, CRP, blood and urine cultures, urinalysis, CXR.
  • ECG (hypo- or hyperkalaemia, MI as the trigger), troponin, lipase or amylase if abdominal pain, pregnancy test.
  • HbA1c, lipids, urine ACR, foot check, retinal screening status once well.
  • New T1DM: islet autoantibodies and C-peptide if the type is unclear. Pancreatic diabetes: CT or MRI pancreas, faecal elastase.

Treatment

DKA (JBDS 2021 principles, follow the local integrated care pathway)

  • Fluids: 0.9% NaCl 1 L over 1 h (500 mL bolus first if SBP <90, and think sepsis or another cause). Then 1 L over 2 h twice, 1 L over 4 h twice, 1 L over 6 h. Add K⁺ per the potassium rule below and adjust for heart failure, the elderly and the young.
  • Fixed-rate IV insulin infusion (FRIII) 0.1 units/kg/h, started at the same time as fluids. Continue the patient's long-acting (basal) insulin alongside it.
  • Potassium: K⁺ >5.5: none. 3.5–5.5: 40 mmol/L in each bag. <3.5: senior review and extra K⁺ (insulin will drop it further).
  • Add 10% glucose at 125 mL/h when glucose <14 mmol/L and continue insulin.
  • Monitor hourly capillary glucose and ketones, VBG at 60 min, 2 h, then 2-hourly. Targets: ketones fall ≥0.5 mmol/L/h, bicarbonate rise ≥3 mmol/L/h, glucose fall ≥3 mmol/L/h. If not met, check the insulin pump, cannula and fluids, and consider raising the insulin rate.
  • Resolved when ketones <0.6 and pH >7.3. Then switch to subcutaneous insulin when eating and drinking (stop the infusion 30–60 min after the first SC dose).
  • Bicarbonate is not routinely given. Give LMWH prophylaxis. Treat the trigger. Diabetes specialist team review before discharge. Stop SGLT2 inhibitors.
  • Complications: hypokalaemia, hypoglycaemia, cerebral oedema (mainly children and young people), ARDS, VTE, fluid overload.

HHS

  • Slow and careful. IV 0.9% NaCl to restore volume, aiming for a glucose fall of ≤5 mmol/L/h and Na⁺ fall not too rapid (cerebral oedema and osmotic demyelination risk).
  • Start low-dose FRIII (0.05 units/kg/h) only if glucose is not falling with fluids, or if ketonaemia is present.
  • LMWH for everyone (high thrombosis risk). Daily foot check. Treat precipitants (infection, MI, stroke).

Hypoglycaemia

  • Conscious and able to swallow: 15–20 g fast-acting carbohydrate (150–200 mL fruit juice, glucose tablets), recheck in 10–15 min and repeat, then a long-acting carbohydrate snack.
  • Unconscious or unable to swallow: IV 10% glucose 150–200 mL over 15 min (or 20% glucose 75–100 mL), or IM glucagon 1 mg if no IV access. Glucagon works poorly in alcohol excess, liver disease and starvation, and after sulfonylureas.
  • Sulfonylurea hypoglycaemia can recur for days. Observe and consider octreotide with specialist advice.

Chronic and inpatient management

  • T1DM: basal-bolus insulin. T2DM: metformin first line, then add SGLT2 inhibitor (especially with CVD, HF or CKD), DPP-4 inhibitor, GLP-1 agonist, sulfonylurea or pioglitazone. Cardiorenal-protective agents are prioritised.
  • Never omit basal insulin in T1DM inpatients. If nil by mouth or unwell, use a variable-rate IV insulin infusion (VRIII) with glucose and K⁺.
  • Hold metformin in AKI, sepsis and after IV contrast (lactic acidosis risk). Sick-day rules: stop SGLT2i, metformin, ACEi/ARB and diuretics when acutely unwell and dehydrated.
  • Pancreatic diabetes: low-dose insulin with caution, pancreatic enzyme replacement, and avoid alcohol.

Emergency protocols and red flags

  • JBDS DKA guideline (adults) and the local DKA integrated care pathway. JBDS HHS and hypoglycaemia guidelines. VRIII pathway for surgical and NBM patients.
  • ABCDE assessment, NEWS2 and Sepsis Six if infection is the trigger.
  • Escalate: severe DKA, GCS <12, K⁺ outside 3.5–5.5 on presentation, pregnancy, HHS, or failure to respond (refer to critical care or the diabetes team).
  • Newly diagnosed T1DM or any DKA needs a diabetes specialist team referral before discharge.
  • DVLA: hypoglycaemia awareness and insulin treatment have driving implications.
Acute coronary syndrome (ACS)Cardiovascular

Background and pathophysiology

  • Umbrella term for STEMI, NSTEMI and unstable angina. Usually atherosclerotic plaque rupture or erosion with platelet aggregation and thrombus (type 1 MI). Type 2 MI is supply–demand mismatch (sepsis, anaemia, tachyarrhythmia, hypotension).
  • STEMI: complete coronary occlusion causing transmural infarction. NSTEMI: partial or transient occlusion, troponin rises. Unstable angina: ischaemia without troponin rise.
  • Risk factors: smoking, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidaemia, family history, CKD, obesity, cocaine (spasm and thrombosis).

Diagnosis and clinical signs

  • Central, crushing or heavy chest pain, lasting >20 min, radiating to jaw or left arm, with sweating, nausea and breathlessness. Atypical or silent presentations are common in older people, women and diabetics (dyspnoea, fatigue, epigastric pain, syncope).
  • Signs: pallor, sweating, tachy- or bradycardia, new murmur (papillary muscle rupture, mitral regurgitation), S3, crackles (heart failure), hypotension and cool peripheries (cardiogenic shock).
  • STEMI on ECG: new ST elevation at the J point in two contiguous leads (roughly ≥1 mm in most leads, ≥2 mm in V2–V3 in men ≥40, ≥1.5 mm in V2–V3 in women). Posterior MI: ST depression V1–V3 with tall R waves, confirm with V7–V9. Also consider hyperacute T waves, de Winter and Wellens patterns.
  • NSTEMI/UA: ST depression, T-wave inversion or a normal ECG. Diagnosis by a rise and fall in high-sensitivity troponin above the 99th percentile.
  • Territories: II, III, aVF = inferior (RCA, may involve right ventricle, check V4R); V1–V4 = anterior (LAD); I, aVL, V5–V6 = lateral (LAD or circumflex).
  • Differentials: PE, aortic dissection, pericarditis, myocarditis, pneumothorax, oesophageal spasm or reflux, Takotsubo, musculoskeletal.

Investigations

  • 12-lead ECG within 10 minutes of arrival, repeat if pain recurs or changes.
  • High-sensitivity troponin on arrival and repeat per local pathway (NICE: at 3 h, or a rapid 0/1–3 h algorithm). A very low value at presentation more than 3 h after pain onset can rule out MI. Troponin can be raised in PE, sepsis, renal failure, myocarditis and HF, so interpret in context.
  • FBC, U&E, glucose, HbA1c, lipids, LFTs, coagulation, CRP, TFTs as indicated.
  • CXR (heart failure, alternative causes), bedside echocardiogram (wall motion, LVEF, mechanical complications).
  • Risk score: GRACE (6-month mortality >3% is higher risk). TIMI is an alternative. Assess bleeding risk (ORBIT, CRUSADE).
  • Coronary angiography ± PCI or CABG.

Treatment

  • Immediate: resuscitation area, monitoring, IV access, aspirin 300 mg chewed. Oxygen only if SpO₂ <94% (target 94–98%). Sublingual or IV GTN for ongoing pain (avoid if hypotensive, right ventricular infarct, or recent PDE-5 inhibitor). IV opioid only for severe pain (may slow absorption of oral antiplatelets).
  • STEMI: emergency reperfusion. Primary PCI if presenting within 12 h and PCI can be delivered within 120 minutes of the time fibrinolysis could have been given. Second antiplatelet (ticagrelor 180 mg, prasugrel or clopidogrel) plus heparin. If PCI cannot be achieved in time: fibrinolysis (e.g. tenecteplase) with an antithrombin, repeat ECG at 60–90 min, rescue PCI if ST elevation has not resolved by ≥50%.
  • NSTEMI/UA: aspirin 300 mg, ticagrelor 180 mg (clopidogrel if high bleeding risk or on an anticoagulant), fondaparinux 2.5 mg SC daily (UFH if angiography imminent or eGFR <20). Angiography within 24–72 h based on risk (immediate if haemodynamically unstable, refractory pain, life-threatening arrhythmia).
  • Secondary prevention: dual antiplatelets for up to 12 months, β-blocker, ACE inhibitor (or ARB), high-intensity statin (atorvastatin 80 mg), MRA if LVEF ≤40% with HF or diabetes, cardiac rehabilitation, smoking cessation, glycaemic and BP control.
  • Complications: VF/VT, AV block (inferior MI), acute heart failure and cardiogenic shock, papillary muscle rupture, VSD, free-wall rupture, pericarditis and Dressler's syndrome, LV aneurysm and thrombus.
  • Driving (DVLA, car): 1 week if treated by successful angioplasty, otherwise 4 weeks. Check the current rules.

Emergency protocols and red flags

  • Local STEMI pathway (call the cath lab or primary PCI centre early). NSTEMI/ACS pathway (NICE CG95 / NG185).
  • ALS algorithm for VF/VT or cardiac arrest. Bradycardia and tachycardia algorithms for arrhythmia complications. Consider temporary pacing in high-grade AV block.
  • Cardiogenic shock: urgent senior and critical care input, echo, consider mechanical support.
  • New hypotension or a new murmur after MI: think mechanical complication, urgent echo and cardiothoracic referral.
Acute kidney injury (AKI)Renal and urinary

Background and pathophysiology

KDIGO definition: creatinine rise ≥26 µmol/L within 48 h, or ≥1.5× baseline within 7 days, or urine output <0.5 mL/kg/h for >6 h. Stage 1 = 1.5–1.9× baseline, stage 2 = 2–2.9×, stage 3 = ≥3× or creatinine >354 µmol/L or need for RRT.
  • Pre-renal (most common): hypovolaemia (bleeding, GI losses, poor intake), sepsis, heart failure, liver failure (hepatorenal), drugs reducing renal perfusion (NSAIDs, ACEi/ARB, diuretics).
  • Intrinsic: acute tubular necrosis (prolonged ischaemia or toxins: aminoglycosides, contrast), acute interstitial nephritis (PPIs, antibiotics, NSAIDs), glomerulonephritis and vasculitis, rhabdomyolysis, myeloma, haemolytic uraemic syndrome.
  • Post-renal: obstruction (BPH, stones, pelvic or bladder tumour, blocked catheter, retroperitoneal fibrosis).
  • Unresolved pre-renal injury becomes ATN. Risk factors: age, CKD, diabetes, heart failure, liver disease, nephrotoxic drugs.

Diagnosis and clinical signs

  • Often asymptomatic, picked up on bloods (and the hospital AKI e-alert). Ask about fluid intake, vomiting or diarrhoea, urine output, LUTS, new drugs (NSAIDs, antibiotics, contrast), rash, joint pain, haematuria.
  • Volume status: postural drop, dry mucosa, reduced skin turgor, low JVP, tachycardia (hypovolaemia) versus raised JVP, oedema, crackles (overload).
  • Palpable bladder or enlarged prostate (obstruction). Rash, purpura, arthritis (vasculitis or SLE). Uraemic signs: pericardial rub, asterixis, confusion, nausea, pruritus.
  • Check for sepsis and consider hepatorenal syndrome in a patient with cirrhosis.

Investigations

  • U&E and creatinine compared with baseline, K⁺, bicarbonate, urea. VBG (acidosis, lactate, K⁺). ECG if K⁺ is raised.
  • Urine dipstick (blood and protein suggest glomerular disease; leucocytes and nitrites suggest infection), urine ACR or PCR, urine microscopy if intrinsic disease is suspected.
  • Bladder scan or catheterise if retention is suspected. Renal ultrasound within 24 h if no clear cause or obstruction suspected (urgently, within 6 h, if pyonephrosis or an obstructed infected system is suspected).
  • FBC, CRP, LFTs, Ca²⁺, CK (rhabdomyolysis), blood cultures if septic.
  • If intrinsic disease is suspected: ANCA, anti-GBM, ANA, dsDNA, complement, immunoglobulins with serum protein electrophoresis and free light chains (myeloma), hepatitis B/C, HIV. Renal biopsy is decided by nephrology.

Treatment

  • Treat the cause. Assess fluid status carefully. If hypovolaemic: IV balanced crystalloid bolus (250–500 mL over 15 min), then reassess; don't overload. If overloaded: diuretics ± escalate.
  • Stop or hold nephrotoxic and renally cleared drugs: NSAIDs, ACEi/ARB, diuretics, metformin, aminoglycosides, and review doses of others. Avoid IV contrast unless essential.
  • Strict fluid balance, daily weights, catheter if hourly urine output needs monitoring. Treat sepsis. Relieve obstruction (urethral catheter, nephrostomy, ureteric stent).
  • Hyperkalaemia (UK Kidney Association principles; K⁺ 5.5–5.9 mild, 6.0–6.4 moderate, ≥6.5 severe):
    • If ECG changes (peaked T waves, flattened P, prolonged PR, wide QRS, sine wave): IV calcium (10% calcium gluconate 30 mL, or 10% calcium chloride 10 mL, over 5–10 min), repeat if ECG changes persist.
    • 10 units short-acting insulin with 25 g glucose IV over 15 min (monitor glucose for 6 h). Nebulised salbutamol 10–20 mg.
    • Potassium binder (sodium zirconium cyclosilicate 10 g TDS for up to 48 h). Stop K⁺-raising drugs. Dialysis if refractory.
  • Indications for urgent renal replacement therapy: refractory hyperkalaemia, refractory pulmonary oedema, severe metabolic acidosis (pH <7.1), uraemic complications (pericarditis, encephalopathy), certain toxins.
  • Specific causes: vasculitis or GN (steroids and immunosuppression via nephrology), AIN (stop the drug, steroids), rhabdomyolysis (aggressive IV fluids), myeloma.
  • Refer to nephrology for stage 3 AKI, suspected intrinsic renal disease, hyperkalaemia refractory to treatment, or failure to respond. Follow up creatinine after discharge (risk of CKD).

Emergency protocols and red flags

  • Hyperkalaemia protocol (UK Kidney Association / local guideline). NICE NG148 (AKI) and the "Think Kidneys" AKI care bundle.
  • Sepsis Six if sepsis is present (see the sepsis factfile). Pulmonary oedema pathway for overload.
  • Urgent decompression for an infected obstructed system. Sick-day rules and medicines review at discharge.
Acute seizures and status epilepticusNeurology

Background and pathophysiology

  • A seizure is a transient episode of abnormal, excessive or synchronous neuronal activity. Imbalance between excitation (glutamate) and inhibition (GABA).
  • Provoked (acute symptomatic): hypoglycaemia, hyponatraemia, hypocalcaemia, hypomagnesaemia, alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal, drugs (tramadol, TCAs, bupropion, cocaine), CNS infection, stroke, head injury, tumour, eclampsia, hypoxia.
  • Unprovoked: epilepsy (two unprovoked seizures more than 24 h apart, or one with a high recurrence risk). Sub-therapeutic anti-seizure medication or missed doses is a common trigger.
  • Status epilepticus: a seizure lasting ≥5 minutes, or two or more seizures without recovery of consciousness between them. Neuronal damage worsens with duration; it is a medical emergency.

Diagnosis and clinical signs

  • A witness history is the most important part. Ask about aura, onset (focal versus generalised), tongue biting (lateral), incontinence, duration, post-ictal state, and Todd's paresis.
  • Generalised tonic-clonic: loss of consciousness, tonic then clonic phases, post-ictal confusion for minutes to hours. Focal: preserved or impaired awareness with focal motor, sensory or automatism features.
  • Convulsive syncope is brief (seconds) with a clear trigger (standing, pain), pallor and rapid recovery. Functional (dissociative) seizures may show eye closure, side-to-side head movement, long duration and rapid recovery, without a raised prolactin or lactate.
  • Examine for focal neurology, meningism, head injury, signs of alcohol excess, and check pupils and glucose.

Investigations

  • Capillary glucose immediately. U&E, Na⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, LFTs, FBC, CRP, VBG (lactate is raised transiently after a tonic-clonic seizure), CK, alcohol level and toxicology, pregnancy test, anti-seizure drug levels where relevant.
  • ECG in every first seizure (long QT, arrhythmic syncope).
  • CT head if new focal deficit, head injury, anticoagulation, immunosuppression, persistent altered consciousness, or first seizure in adulthood with red flags. LP if meningitis or encephalitis suspected (after CT if indicated).
  • EEG and MRI are usually arranged by neurology, within 72 h if status or in hospital as indicated.

Treatment

  • First aid: note the time, protect from injury, nothing in the mouth, recovery position after convulsions stop, oxygen (high flow) and monitoring, check glucose, gain IV access.
  • Status epilepticus (seizure ≥5 min): first-line IV lorazepam 4 mg (0.1 mg/kg), repeat once after 10 min if still seizing. If no IV access: buccal midazolam 10 mg (or IM midazolam) or rectal diazepam 10 mg.
  • Second line (if seizing 20 min after starting first-line): levetiracetam 60 mg/kg IV (max 4.5 g) over 10 min, or phenytoin 20 mg/kg IV (max 2 g, ≤50 mg/min with ECG and BP monitoring), or sodium valproate 40 mg/kg IV (max 3 g, avoid in women of childbearing potential).
  • Refractory status: ICU, general anaesthesia (propofol, midazolam or thiopentone) with EEG monitoring.
  • Correct causes: 10% glucose for hypoglycaemia, IV thiamine (Pabrinex) in alcohol misuse, electrolyte correction, magnesium sulfate 4 g IV over 5–10 min then infusion for eclampsia, antibiotics and aciclovir if CNS infection suspected.
  • First seizure that has resolved: no routine anti-seizure drug started in the ward. Refer to a first-seizure clinic (neurology within 2 weeks). Counsel on driving (DVLA: usually 6 months off after a first unprovoked seizure, 12 months for epilepsy), bathing, heights and water.

Emergency protocols and red flags

  • Status epilepticus algorithm (local and NICE guidance). ABCDE and NEWS2. Escalate to critical care early if not responding to second-line drugs.
  • Eclampsia: magnesium sulfate, obstetric emergency team.
  • Head injury, anticoagulation, or a fever or meningism with seizure: image and treat as CNS infection until proven otherwise (see the sepsis factfile).
  • Alcohol-related seizures: see the drugs factfile. Safeguarding and capacity considerations where relevant.
AnaphylaxisGeneral acute

Background and pathophysiology

  • Severe, life-threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction. Usually IgE-mediated: allergen cross-links IgE on mast cells and basophils, releasing histamine, tryptase and leukotrienes. Results in vasodilation, capillary leak, bronchospasm and laryngeal oedema.
  • Non-IgE mechanisms (direct mast-cell activation) occur with NSAIDs, opioids, IV contrast and some neuromuscular blockers.
  • Triggers: food (nuts, shellfish, milk, egg), drugs (β-lactams, NSAIDs, neuromuscular blockers, chlorhexidine, contrast), venom (wasp, bee), latex. Around 1 in 3 have no identified trigger. Higher risk with asthma, mastocytosis, β-blockers or ACEi, and exercise or alcohol as co-factors.

Diagnosis and clinical signs

Likely anaphylaxis when all are present: sudden onset and rapid progression, life-threatening airway and/or breathing and/or circulation problems, and skin or mucosal changes (flushing, urticaria, angioedema). Skin changes can be absent in up to 20% and may not appear until after BP falls.
  • A: hoarse voice, stridor, throat tightness, swollen tongue or lips. B: wheeze, breathlessness, cyanosis, hypoxia. C: tachycardia, hypotension, pallor, collapse. D: confusion, agitation, sense of doom, reduced consciousness.
  • Also GI symptoms (vomiting, cramps, diarrhoea). Exposure to a known trigger in the previous minutes to hours.
  • Differentials: vasovagal syncope, panic attack, asthma, hereditary angioedema (no urticaria, no response to adrenaline), septic or cardiogenic shock, PE.

Investigations

  • Do not delay treatment for tests. Anaphylaxis is a clinical diagnosis.
  • Mast cell tryptase: first sample as soon as possible, second 1–2 h after onset (no later than 4 h), third at ≥24 h (baseline, or at follow-up). A raised acute level relative to baseline supports the diagnosis; a normal level does not exclude it.
  • Bedside: SpO₂, ABG if severe, ECG.
  • Referral to an allergy clinic for every case (identify the trigger, provide adrenaline auto-injectors, training and a written action plan).

Treatment

  • Remove the trigger if possible (stop the infusion), call for help, note the time.
  • IM adrenaline 1 mg/mL (1:1000), anterolateral mid-thigh: adult and child >12 y 500 mcg (0.5 mL); 6–12 y 300 mcg (0.3 mL); 6 months–6 y 150 mcg (0.15 mL); <6 months 100–150 mcg. Repeat every 5 minutes if there's no improvement.
  • Position: lie flat with legs raised (sit up if breathing is difficult, left lateral if pregnant). Do not stand or walk the patient up.
  • High-flow oxygen (15 L/min via reservoir mask). IV fluid bolus 500–1000 mL crystalloid for hypotension.
  • Antihistamines and steroids are not part of initial resuscitation (RCUK 2021) and do not treat airway, breathing or circulation problems. They can be used later for skin symptoms.
  • Refractory anaphylaxis (no response after 2 IM doses): call critical care or anaesthetics for an IV adrenaline infusion (specialist setting), further fluids, and glucagon 1–2 mg IV if on a β-blocker.
  • Observe for a minimum of 6–12 h depending on severity (longer after severe reactions or if more than one adrenaline dose was needed; biphasic reactions can occur). Discharge with two adrenaline auto-injectors, training, avoidance advice and allergy referral.

Emergency protocols and red flags

  • Resuscitation Council UK anaphylaxis algorithm (ABCDE, adrenaline first). Call 2222 if airway compromise, refractory shock or collapse.
  • Cardiac arrest: follow ALS and give IM or IV adrenaline as per algorithm.
  • Report suspected drug reactions to the MHRA Yellow Card scheme and record the allergy in the notes and on the drug chart.
Asthma (acute attack)Respiratory

Background and pathophysiology

  • Chronic inflammatory airway disease with variable, reversible airflow obstruction and bronchial hyper-responsiveness. Type 2 (allergic) inflammation with eosinophils, IgE and mast cells is typical; airway smooth muscle constriction, mucus plugging and oedema narrow the lumen.
  • Triggers: viral respiratory infection, allergens, exercise, cold air, smoke, pollution, NSAIDs and aspirin, β-blockers, emotional stress. Poor adherence to inhaled corticosteroid is a common cause of admission.
  • Risk factors for fatal asthma: previous ICU admission or ventilation, repeated admissions, heavy reliance on SABA, poor adherence, psychosocial problems.

Diagnosis and clinical signs

BTS/SIGN severity:
Moderate: increasing symptoms, PEF 50–75% of best or predicted, no severe features.
Acute severe (any one): PEF 33–50%, RR ≥25, HR ≥110, unable to complete sentences in one breath.
Life-threatening (any one): PEF <33%, SpO₂ <92%, PaO₂ <8 kPa, "normal" PaCO₂ (4.6–6.0 kPa), silent chest, cyanosis, poor respiratory effort, arrhythmia, exhaustion, altered consciousness, hypotension.
Near-fatal: raised PaCO₂ and/or need for mechanical ventilation.
  • Signs: wheeze, tachypnoea, tachycardia, accessory muscle use, hyperinflation, pulsus paradoxus. A silent chest is ominous, not reassuring. A rising or normal CO₂ in an acute severe attack means the patient is tiring.
  • Look for the trigger (infection, ICS non-adherence) and for complications (pneumothorax, pneumonia, lobar collapse).
  • Differentials: COPD exacerbation, pulmonary oedema, PE, pneumothorax, anaphylaxis, upper airway obstruction, vocal cord dysfunction.

Investigations

  • Peak expiratory flow (compare with personal best or predicted), SpO₂, RR, HR, ability to speak.
  • ABG if SpO₂ <92% or any life-threatening feature. Pay attention to PaCO₂ and pH.
  • CXR if life-threatening features, suspected pneumothorax or consolidation, or failure to respond.
  • U&E (K⁺ can fall with high-dose salbutamol), FBC, CRP, and theophylline level if on it.

Treatment

  • Oxygen to keep SpO₂ 94–98%.
  • Nebulised salbutamol 5 mg (oxygen-driven), repeated every 15–30 min or continuously in life-threatening asthma. Add ipratropium 500 mcg nebulised for severe or life-threatening attacks.
  • Steroids early: prednisolone 40–50 mg orally (or hydrocortisone 100 mg IV if unable to swallow), continue for 5 days.
  • Magnesium sulfate 1.2–2 g IV over 20 min (single dose) for acute severe asthma not responding to initial bronchodilators, and for life-threatening asthma.
  • Life-threatening or not responding: senior and ICU review early. IV aminophylline or IV salbutamol only on senior advice. Intubation is high risk and done by an expert. NIV is not routine.
  • Repeat PEF 15–30 min after starting treatment. Monitor K⁺, ABG and lactate (salbutamol can cause type B lactic acidosis).
  • Discharge criteria: PEF >75% of best or predicted, stable on discharge medication for 12–24 h. Check inhaler technique, give a written personal asthma action plan, a course of oral steroids, and follow up with GP within 2 working days and respiratory clinic within 4 weeks. Assess for psychosocial and adherence problems.
  • Chronic management follows the 2024 joint BTS/NICE/SIGN guideline: ICS-containing regimens, with an as-needed ICS-formoterol reliever (MART) for most adults instead of a SABA-only reliever.

Emergency protocols and red flags

  • BTS/SIGN acute asthma algorithm and the asthma attack care bundle. ABCDE, NEWS2.
  • ICU referral for a life-threatening attack, deteriorating PEF, rising PaCO₂, exhaustion, or altered consciousness. Never sedate.
  • Anaphylaxis can present as asthma. Consider it if there is urticaria, angioedema or hypotension.
Cancer: new presentations, complications and treatment toxicityHaem and oncology

Background and pathophysiology

  • Cancer arises from accumulated genetic and epigenetic changes leading to uncontrolled proliferation, evasion of apoptosis and invasion or metastasis. Common acute presentations relate to the primary, to metastases, to systemic effects (paraneoplastic), or to treatment.
  • New presentation: often non-specific. Unexplained weight loss, fatigue, night sweats, persistent pain, change in bowel habit, PR or PV bleeding, haemoptysis, dysphagia, iron-deficiency anaemia, lumps, new-onset venous thrombosis, or an emergency (obstruction, perforation, MSCC, SVCO, pleural effusion).
  • Cancer of unknown primary (CUP): metastases with no primary after standard workup. Managed by the CUP MDT.
  • Paraneoplastic: hypercalcaemia (PTHrP), SIADH (small-cell lung cancer), Lambert–Eaton, Cushing's (ectopic ACTH), hypercoagulability (Trousseau's), dermatomyositis, acanthosis nigricans.
  • Treatment types: surgery, radiotherapy, cytotoxic chemotherapy, targeted therapy, hormone therapy, immunotherapy (immune checkpoint inhibitors, CAR-T).

Diagnosis and clinical signs

  • Follow NICE NG12 suspected-cancer referral criteria (urgent suspected cancer pathway, 28-day faster diagnosis standard). Ask about family history, smoking, occupational exposures, weight loss, and performance status (ECOG 0–4).
  • Examine for lymphadenopathy, masses, hepatomegaly, ascites, bone tenderness, skin lesions, pleural effusion, and neurology.
  • Neutropenic sepsis: temperature >38 °C (or other signs of sepsis) with neutrophils ≤0.5 × 10⁹/L (or expected to fall) in a patient on chemotherapy. May be hypothermic or afebrile if very unwell.
  • Metastatic spinal cord compression (MSCC): new back pain (thoracic, nocturnal, worse lying flat, or radicular), then limb weakness, sensory level, sphincter dysfunction. Any patient with cancer and new back pain needs assessment.
  • Superior vena cava obstruction (SVCO): facial and neck swelling, dyspnoea, engorged neck veins, plethora, Pemberton's sign; often lung cancer or lymphoma.
  • Hypercalcaemia of malignancy: thirst, polyuria, constipation, nausea, abdominal pain, confusion, short QT; corrected calcium >2.6 mmol/L (severe >3.5).
  • Tumour lysis syndrome: after chemotherapy in high-turnover tumours (leukaemia, lymphoma). ↑K⁺, ↑phosphate, ↓Ca²⁺, ↑urate, AKI, arrhythmia.
  • Brain metastases: headache, seizures, focal deficit, confusion. Malignant bowel obstruction, malignant pleural or pericardial effusion (tamponade), VTE and hyponatraemia (SIADH) are other frequent acute problems.
  • Treatment-related toxicity: chemotherapy (neutropenia, anaemia, thrombocytopenia, mucositis, diarrhoea, nausea, cardiotoxicity from anthracyclines and trastuzumab, nephrotoxicity from cisplatin, pulmonary toxicity from bleomycin, neuropathy from vincristine or oxaliplatin, extravasation); radiotherapy (mucositis, pneumonitis, skin, fatigue); hormonal therapy.
  • Immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) toxicity (pembrolizumab, nivolumab, ipilimumab, atezolizumab, durvalumab): immune-related adverse events (irAEs) can affect any organ, from weeks to months (and after stopping) treatment.
    • Colitis: diarrhoea, abdominal pain, blood or mucus (may perforate). Pneumonitis: cough, dyspnoea, hypoxia, ground-glass change on CT. Hepatitis: raised ALT, often asymptomatic.
    • Endocrine: hypothyroidism or thyrotoxicosis, hypophysitis (headache, fatigue, low cortisol), adrenal insufficiency, new T1DM or DKA. Dermatitis, nephritis, myocarditis (troponin, arrhythmia; high mortality), neurological (myasthenia, Guillain–Barré, encephalitis), arthritis.

Investigations

  • General: FBC, U&E, LFTs, bone profile (corrected Ca²⁺), LDH, CRP, clotting, bone and lipid profile as relevant. Tumour markers only when guided (PSA, CA-125, AFP, β-hCG, CEA, myeloma screen); they are not screening tests.
  • CT chest, abdomen and pelvis for staging. Biopsy for histology is essential before treatment. PET-CT and MRI as directed. Discuss at the MDT.
  • Neutropenic sepsis: blood cultures (peripheral and from every line lumen), lactate, urine and sputum culture, CXR, FBC with differential, CRP, U&E, LFTs.
  • MSCC: urgent MRI whole spine (within 24 h if neurological symptoms or signs, within 1 week if pain suggests spinal metastases without neurology).
  • Hypercalcaemia: corrected Ca²⁺, PTH (suppressed in malignancy), PTHrP, vitamin D, U&E, ECG.
  • Suspected irAE: exclude infection first (stool culture, C. difficile, CT, cultures), then organ-specific tests (TFTs, cortisol and ACTH, glucose, troponin and ECG, LFTs, CK, CT chest, endoscopy or biopsy) and early acute oncology advice.

Treatment

  • New presentation: stabilise, symptom control, diagnostic pathway, MDT, and discuss prognosis and goals honestly. Involve palliative care early for symptoms, advance care planning and DNACPR or ReSPECT discussions.
  • Neutropenic sepsis: treat as an emergency. Broad-spectrum IV antibiotics (piperacillin-tazobactam unless allergic) within 1 hour per local policy, plus resuscitation. Don't wait for the neutrophil count if a patient on chemotherapy is unwell. MASCC score helps risk-stratify. G-CSF at oncology's discretion.
  • MSCC: dexamethasone 16 mg (with gastric protection) as soon as suspected in patients with neurological symptoms, unless lymphoma is suspected (discuss with oncology or haematology first). Contact the acute oncology or MSCC coordinator immediately. Flat bed rest and spinal precautions until stability is assessed. Definitive treatment: radiotherapy or surgery.
  • SVCO: sit upright, oxygen, dexamethasone if lymphoma is likely, urgent CT, oncology for stenting, radiotherapy or chemotherapy.
  • Hypercalcaemia: IV 0.9% NaCl (3–4 L over 24 h if not overloaded), then IV bisphosphonate (zoledronic acid 4 mg or pamidronate) once rehydrated. Stop calcium and vitamin D and thiazides. Calcitonin for rapid effect. Denosumab if bisphosphonate contraindicated.
  • Tumour lysis: aggressive IV hydration, rasburicase (or allopurinol), K⁺ and Ca²⁺ management, renal replacement if required. Call haematology or oncology and critical care.
  • Brain metastases: dexamethasone (8–16 mg) with PPI, anti-seizure drugs if seizures, neurosurgery or oncology.
  • ICI toxicity: grade the toxicity, hold the ICI, contact the acute oncology service. Grade 2: prednisolone 0.5–1 mg/kg. Grade 3–4: admission, IV methylprednisolone 1–2 mg/kg. Refractory colitis: infliximab or vedolizumab. Refractory hepatitis or pneumonitis: mycophenolate or infliximab under specialist care. Endocrinopathies: replace hormones (replace cortisol before thyroxine). ICI myocarditis: high-dose steroids and cardiology.
  • Symptom management: WHO analgesic ladder, antiemetics matched to cause, laxatives with opioids, PPI with steroids, nutrition and dietitian input.

Emergency protocols and red flags

  • Acute oncology service (AOS): 24/7 advice. Patients on systemic anticancer therapy carry an alert card and a 24-hour helpline number. Use the UKONS triage tool.
  • NICE CG151 neutropenic sepsis: antibiotics within 1 hour of arrival. NICE NG234 spinal metastases and MSCC. NICE NG12 suspected cancer referral.
  • Any patient within about 12 months of ICI who becomes unwell (diarrhoea, breathlessness, headache, hypotension, hyperglycaemia) needs an irAE in the differential.
  • Sepsis Six, ABCDE, and VTE prophylaxis (high risk). End of life care: NICE NG31, ReSPECT and DNACPR.
Cerebrovascular disease: TIA and stroke (ischaemic and haemorrhagic)Neurology

Background and pathophysiology

  • Ischaemic stroke (~85%): large-artery atherothrombosis (carotid, intracranial), cardioembolism (AF, MI, valve disease, endocarditis, PFO), small-vessel (lacunar) disease, or less common causes (dissection, vasculitis, hypercoagulability).
  • Haemorrhagic stroke: intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH; hypertension, cerebral amyloid angiopathy, anticoagulants, AVM, tumour) and subarachnoid haemorrhage (SAH; ruptured berry aneurysm).
  • TIA: transient focal neurological deficit due to focal ischaemia without infarction (typically minutes to under 1 h). A high early stroke risk means it should be treated as an emergency.
  • Ischaemic core plus surrounding penumbra (salvageable tissue): treatment aims to restore flow quickly ("time is brain"). Risk factors: hypertension, AF, diabetes, smoking, hyperlipidaemia, carotid stenosis, age.

Diagnosis and clinical signs

  • FAST (Face, Arm, Speech, Time) and ROSIER for recognition. Score severity with the NIHSS.
  • Oxford classification (ischaemic): TACS (weakness, higher cortical dysfunction such as dysphasia or neglect, and hemianopia); PACS (two of those); LACS (pure motor, pure sensory, sensorimotor, or ataxic hemiparesis); POCS (cranial nerve palsy with contralateral motor or sensory deficit, cerebellar signs, isolated hemianopia, brainstem signs).
  • Haemorrhage: sudden headache, vomiting, reduced consciousness, rapid deterioration. SAH: thunderclap headache ("worst of my life"), neck stiffness, photophobia, third-nerve palsy, collapse or seizure.
  • Stroke mimics: hypoglycaemia, post-ictal Todd's paresis, migraine aura, sepsis unmasking old deficits, functional disorders, subdural haematoma, tumour, Bell's palsy.
  • Establish the time last known well (critical for thrombolysis and thrombectomy eligibility), medications (anticoagulants!), and pre-morbid function.

Investigations

  • Capillary glucose first. Then non-contrast CT head immediately for suspected acute stroke (within 1 h at the latest, immediately if thrombolysis candidate, on anticoagulants, GCS <13, or other high-risk features). CT angiography (head and neck) ± perfusion for thrombectomy assessment.
  • ECG (AF), FBC, U&E, glucose, lipids, clotting, ESR/CRP (giant cell arteritis if age >50 with visual symptoms), troponin, blood cultures if endocarditis is suspected.
  • TIA: specialist assessment within 24 h. MRI (DWI) helps. Carotid imaging (Doppler or CTA) for anterior circulation events. Echocardiogram and prolonged ECG monitoring for cardioembolic source.
  • SAH: CT within 6 h is very sensitive. If CT is negative and SAH is still suspected, lumbar puncture at least 12 h after onset (xanthochromia). CT angiography to find the aneurysm.
  • Bedside swallow screen within 4 h of admission. Keep NBM until done.

Treatment

  • Hyperacute stroke unit care. Maintain oxygen only if hypoxic, glucose 4–11 mmol/L, treat fever, position, early mobilisation, IPC for VTE prevention.
  • Thrombolysis (if CT excludes haemorrhage and no contraindication): alteplase 0.9 mg/kg (max 90 mg; 10% bolus, remainder over 60 min) or tenecteplase 0.25 mg/kg single bolus (max 25 mg), within 4.5 h of onset (extended windows with advanced imaging selection). BP must be <185/110 before and <180/105 for 24 h after.
  • Mechanical thrombectomy for anterior circulation large-vessel occlusion, within 6 h of onset (up to 24 h with perfusion imaging selection), in addition to thrombolysis where eligible.
  • Antithrombotics: aspirin 300 mg for 14 days after excluding haemorrhage (delayed 24 h after thrombolysis and repeat imaging), then long-term clopidogrel 75 mg. TIA: aspirin 300 mg immediately and refer. Short-course dual antiplatelets for minor stroke or high-risk TIA per local protocol.
  • AF: anticoagulate (DOAC) once imaging is reviewed, with timing by infarct size (stroke team decision).
  • Secondary prevention: atorvastatin 20–80 mg, BP lowering (after the acute phase), diabetes control, smoking cessation, carotid endarterectomy for 50–99% symptomatic stenosis (ideally within 2 weeks).
  • ICH: reverse anticoagulation (warfarin: vitamin K 5–10 mg IV plus prothrombin complex concentrate; dabigatran: idarucizumab; factor Xa inhibitors: andexanet alfa or PCC). Intensive BP lowering (target SBP ~130–140 within hours). Neurosurgical referral for cerebellar haematoma, hydrocephalus, or surgically accessible clots. Stop antithrombotics.
  • SAH: neurosurgical emergency. Secure the aneurysm (coiling or clipping), nimodipine 60 mg 4-hourly to prevent vasospasm, BP control, keep euvolaemic, watch for hydrocephalus, hyponatraemia and re-bleeding.
  • Complications: haemorrhagic transformation, cerebral oedema, aspiration pneumonia, seizures, VTE, depression, pressure sores. DVLA advice (car: usually 1 month off after stroke or TIA).

Emergency protocols and red flags

  • Hyperacute stroke pathway (call the stroke team, pre-alert). Thrombolysis and thrombectomy criteria per local protocol. Anticoagulant reversal guidelines.
  • SAH pathway and neurosurgical on-call. NICE NG128 (stroke and TIA).
  • Deterioration in GCS after stroke: repeat CT (haemorrhage, oedema, hydrocephalus), check glucose, sodium, and sepsis. Do not lower BP aggressively in acute ischaemic stroke unless thrombolysing or >220/120.
  • Do not give aspirin until haemorrhage is excluded.
Chronic kidney disease (CKD)Renal and urinary

Background and pathophysiology

  • Definition: abnormal kidney structure or function present for >3 months. eGFR <60 mL/min/1.73 m² and/or markers of damage (albuminuria, haematuria, structural abnormality). Staged G1–G5 by eGFR and A1–A3 by ACR.
  • Causes: diabetic nephropathy, hypertension and vascular disease, glomerulonephritis, polycystic kidney disease, obstructive uropathy and reflux, chronic interstitial nephritis, drugs (NSAIDs, lithium, PPIs), myeloma.
  • Pathophysiology: nephron loss triggers hyperfiltration in the remaining nephrons, glomerulosclerosis and progressive scarring, driven by RAAS activation and proteinuria.
  • Complications: hypertension, cardiovascular disease (leading cause of death), anaemia (↓erythropoietin), mineral and bone disease (↓vitamin D activation, ↑phosphate, ↑PTH), metabolic acidosis, hyperkalaemia, fluid overload, uraemia, drug accumulation.

Diagnosis and clinical signs

  • Often asymptomatic until eGFR <15–20. Late: fatigue, anorexia, nausea, pruritus, nocturia, restless legs, oedema, breathlessness, altered taste.
  • Signs: hypertension, pallor, oedema, raised JVP, pericardial rub, asterixis, uraemic frost (late), signs of the underlying disease (diabetic changes, PKD masses, fistula scar).
  • Ask about dialysis: type (haemodialysis or peritoneal), days and access (AV fistula, tunnelled line, PD catheter), transplant status.
  • In hospital: always compare with the patient's baseline creatinine to spot AKI on CKD.

Investigations

  • eGFR (CKD-EPI, creatinine-based) and urine ACR (>3 mg/mmol abnormal; ≥30 is A3). Confirm with repeat testing within 3 months.
  • Urine dipstick (haematuria), BP, HbA1c, lipids, FBC, bone profile, phosphate, PTH, vitamin D, bicarbonate, ferritin and transferrin saturation.
  • Renal ultrasound: small echogenic kidneys typical; large cystic kidneys in PKD; asymmetry raises renovascular disease; hydronephrosis suggests obstruction.
  • Immunology and biopsy (through nephrology) if haematuria with proteinuria, nephrotic syndrome or unexplained rapid decline.
  • Kidney Failure Risk Equation (KFRE): 5-year risk of kidney failure >5% triggers specialist referral.

Treatment

  • Slow progression and reduce CV risk: BP target <140/90 (<130/80 if ACR ≥70). ACEi or ARB if ACR ≥30 with diabetes, ACR ≥70, or hypertension (titrate, monitor U&E at 1–2 weeks; accept a creatinine rise <30%). SGLT2 inhibitor (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin) if eligible. Statin (atorvastatin 20 mg). Smoking cessation and glycaemic control.
  • Anaemia: exclude iron and B12 or folate deficiency, replace iron, then erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (target Hb 100–120 g/L).
  • Mineral and bone: phosphate binders, dietary phosphate restriction, active vitamin D (alfacalcidol), calcimimetics under specialist advice.
  • Acidosis: oral sodium bicarbonate if persistent and bicarbonate low. Avoid nephrotoxins (NSAIDs, contrast if possible) and dose-adjust renally cleared drugs.
  • Referral (NICE NG203): eGFR <30, ACR ≥70 (unless due to diabetes and appropriately treated), sustained fall in eGFR (≥25% with change in category, or ≥15 mL/min/1.73 m² per year), 5-year KFRE >5%, haematuria with proteinuria, suspected rare cause, resistant hypertension.
  • Renal replacement therapy: haemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, transplantation, or conservative kidney management. Vaccinations: influenza, pneumococcus, hepatitis B.
  • Dialysis patients on the ward: protect the fistula arm (no BP cuff, cannula, or blood tests), watch potassium and fluid status, liaise with the renal unit about dialysis schedules, and dose drugs carefully.

Emergency protocols and red flags

  • Hyperkalaemia (see the AKI factfile) and pulmonary oedema pathways. Missed dialysis plus symptoms means urgent renal team contact.
  • AKI on CKD: follow the AKI care bundle. Sick-day rules education (hold ACEi/ARB, diuretics, metformin, SGLT2i, NSAIDs during acute illness).
  • Uraemic pericarditis, encephalopathy, refractory hyperkalaemia or overload: urgent dialysis.
  • Avoid gadolinium and unnecessary contrast at low eGFR. NICE NG203 (CKD).
Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD)Respiratory

Background and pathophysiology

  • Persistent, usually progressive airflow obstruction (post-bronchodilator FEV₁/FVC <0.7) that is not fully reversible. Main cause is smoking; also occupational dusts and fumes, biomass smoke, and α1-antitrypsin deficiency (young, non-smokers, basal emphysema, family history).
  • Chronic bronchitis (mucus hypersecretion and cough) plus emphysema (alveolar destruction, loss of elastic recoil, air trapping).
  • Pathophysiology: neutrophilic and macrophage inflammation, small-airway narrowing, V/Q mismatch, hyperinflation. Advanced disease leads to hypoxia, chronic CO₂ retention (driven mainly by V/Q mismatch and reduced ventilatory drive or capacity, not simply loss of "hypoxic drive") and cor pulmonale.
  • Acute exacerbation: sustained worsening of dyspnoea, cough, sputum volume or purulence. Triggers: viral or bacterial infection, pollution, non-adherence. Others to exclude: pneumonia, pneumothorax, PE, heart failure, arrhythmia.

Diagnosis and clinical signs

  • Chronic breathlessness (grade with MRC dyspnoea scale), cough, sputum, wheeze, recurrent chest infections.
  • Signs: barrel chest, hyperinflation with reduced cricosternal distance, hyperresonance, quiet breath sounds, pursed-lip breathing, accessory muscle use, wheeze, prolonged expiration, cyanosis, cor pulmonale (raised JVP, peripheral oedema), CO₂ retention (flapping tremor, bounding pulse, drowsiness, headache).
  • Diagnosis is confirmed with spirometry when stable, not during an exacerbation.
  • Risk of hypercapnic respiratory failure: known COPD, previous type 2 respiratory failure, obesity hypoventilation, neuromuscular disease.

Investigations

  • SpO₂ and ABG (record the inspired oxygen). Look at pH and PaCO₂. Repeat within 30–60 minutes of any change in oxygen.
  • CXR (pneumonia, pneumothorax, hyperinflation, bullae, heart size, effusion), ECG (AF, multifocal atrial tachycardia, right heart strain), FBC (eosinophils, polycythaemia), U&E, CRP, blood glucose, sputum culture if purulent, blood cultures if septic.
  • Consider troponin, BNP, D-dimer or CTPA if the picture doesn't fit.
  • When stable: spirometry, full PFTs, α1-antitrypsin level (once), echo if cor pulmonale is suspected.

Treatment

  • Controlled oxygen: target SpO₂ 88–92% (Venturi mask 24% or 28%, or nasal cannulae 1–2 L/min) until ABG results are known. Give an oxygen alert card. Avoid under-treating hypoxia.
  • Nebulised bronchodilators: salbutamol 2.5–5 mg ± ipratropium 500 mcg, air-driven (with supplemental oxygen via nasal cannulae) if hypercapnic or acidotic.
  • Prednisolone 30 mg daily for 5 days.
  • Antibiotics if purulent sputum or clinical signs of pneumonia, per local policy (e.g. amoxicillin 500 mg TDS, doxycycline 200 mg then 100 mg, or clarithromycin), usually 5 days.
  • NIV if respiratory acidosis persists (pH <7.35 and PaCO₂ >6.5 kPa) after 1 hour of optimal medical therapy. Recheck ABG at 1 h and 4 h. If NIV fails, discuss invasive ventilation or palliation in advance, which requires a clear treatment escalation plan.
  • Other measures: VTE prophylaxis, physiotherapy, nutritional support, treat heart failure and arrhythmia, IV aminophylline only on senior advice.
  • Prevention: smoking cessation, inhaler review and technique (LABA/LAMA, add ICS if asthmatic features, eosinophils ≥300 or frequent exacerbations), pulmonary rehabilitation (within 4 weeks of discharge), vaccination (influenza, pneumococcal, COVID-19, RSV as offered), rescue pack, long-term oxygen therapy if PaO₂ <7.3 kPa (or <8 kPa with pulmonary hypertension, polycythaemia, or oedema), self-management plan.
  • Discuss escalation and ceiling of care, advance care planning and palliative needs in advanced disease.

Emergency protocols and red flags

  • BTS emergency oxygen guideline (88–92% target for those at risk of hypercapnia). Local NIV protocol and treatment escalation plan. NICE NG115 (COPD) and NG114 (exacerbation antibiotics).
  • Drowsy on oxygen: think CO₂ retention. Check ABG and reduce oxygen to target, don't remove it.
  • Sudden worsening: exclude pneumothorax (bullae), PE, arrhythmia and pneumonia. ICU referral criteria include failed NIV and severe acidosis.
Cirrhosis and liver decompensationGastro and liver

Background and pathophysiology

  • Chronic liver injury leads to fibrosis and regenerative nodules with distorted architecture. Causes: alcohol, MASLD (metabolic-associated fatty liver disease), chronic hepatitis B or C, autoimmune (AIH, PBC, PSC), haemochromatosis, Wilson's disease, α1-antitrypsin deficiency, drugs, cardiac failure.
  • Two consequences: portal hypertension (varices, ascites, splenomegaly, hepatorenal syndrome) and impaired synthetic and detoxifying function (coagulopathy, low albumin, jaundice, encephalopathy).
  • Compensated cirrhosis may be asymptomatic. Decompensation: ascites, variceal bleeding, hepatic encephalopathy, jaundice. Severity: Child–Pugh and MELD-Na.
  • Complications: infection (SBP), AKI and HRS, hepatopulmonary syndrome, portal vein thrombosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).

Diagnosis and clinical signs

  • Chronic liver disease stigmata: jaundice, spider naevi, palmar erythema, gynaecomastia, testicular atrophy, caput medusae, leuconychia, clubbing, Dupuytren's contracture, parotid enlargement, bruising, muscle wasting.
  • Decompensation: ascites (shifting dullness), oedema, splenomegaly, asterixis, confusion, fetor hepaticus, haematemesis or melaena.
  • Hepatic encephalopathy (West Haven): grade 1 (sleep reversal, mild confusion), 2 (drowsy, asterixis), 3 (stupor, disorientated), 4 (coma). Precipitants: infection, GI bleed, constipation, dehydration, electrolyte disturbance (↓K⁺, ↓Na⁺), sedatives, AKI, TIPSS.
  • Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP): may present with only fever, abdominal pain, worsening ascites, encephalopathy or AKI, or with no symptoms.
  • Alcoholic hepatitis: jaundice, fever, tender hepatomegaly, leucocytosis, AST:ALT >2 with AST <300.
  • Hepatorenal syndrome (HRS-AKI): AKI in cirrhosis with no response to volume expansion and diuretic withdrawal, no structural kidney disease.

Investigations

  • LFTs (bilirubin, ALT/AST, ALP, GGT, albumin), INR, FBC (low platelets), U&E and Na⁺, glucose, CRP, VBG and lactate. Cultures (blood, urine, ascitic).
  • Diagnostic paracentesis for every patient admitted with ascites (new or worsening): ascitic fluid neutrophil count (≥250/mm³ = SBP), culture in blood culture bottles at the bedside, protein, albumin (calculate SAAG; ≥11 g/L = portal hypertension).
  • Aetiology screen: viral serology (HBV, HCV), autoantibodies (ANA, ASMA, AMA), immunoglobulins, ferritin and transferrin saturation, caeruloplasmin, α1-antitrypsin, alcohol history (AUDIT).
  • Liver ultrasound with Doppler (portal vein patency, HCC), AFP. FibroScan or ELF. Upper GI endoscopy for varices. CT or MRI if HCC suspected. Alcoholic hepatitis: Maddrey's discriminant function ≥32 or GAHS ≥9 means severe.

Treatment

  • Ascites: sodium restriction (~2 g/day), spironolactone 100 mg ± furosemide 40 mg (100:40 ratio), stop NSAIDs, ACEi and ARBs. Tense ascites: large-volume paracentesis with IV albumin 20% 8 g per litre drained (if >5 L). Refractory ascites: TIPSS or transplant assessment.
  • SBP: IV antibiotics (per local policy, e.g. piperacillin-tazobactam or a third-generation cephalosporin) plus IV albumin 1.5 g/kg on day 1 and 1 g/kg on day 3 to reduce renal failure. Secondary prophylaxis afterwards.
  • Variceal bleeding: see the GI bleeding factfile (terlipressin, prophylactic antibiotics, endoscopic band ligation).
  • Hepatic encephalopathy: treat the precipitant, lactulose (titrate to 2–3 soft stools/day; enemas if needed), add rifaximin 550 mg BD for recurrence. Airway protection for grade 3–4. Avoid sedatives.
  • HRS-AKI: stop diuretics and nephrotoxins, IV albumin (1 g/kg/day, max 100 g), terlipressin with critical care or hepatology input.
  • Alcoholic hepatitis: abstinence, nutrition, thiamine, treat infection. If severe, prednisolone 40 mg for 28 days after excluding sepsis and GI bleeding (assess response at day 7 with the Lille score).
  • General: paracetamol up to 2 g/day is acceptable, avoid NSAIDs and sedatives, correct electrolytes and glucose, replace thiamine, high-protein diet with late-evening snack, vaccinations, alcohol support. Do not correct INR with FFP unless bleeding or a procedure (INR does not reflect bleeding risk in cirrhosis).
  • Surveillance: HCC (ultrasound ± AFP 6-monthly), varices screening. Refer for transplant assessment when MELD ≥15 or after a decompensating event.

Emergency protocols and red flags

  • Decompensated cirrhosis care bundle (BSG/BASL): diagnostic paracentesis, cultures and antibiotics within hours, avoid harmful drugs, alcohol withdrawal prophylaxis, nutrition. Variceal bleed protocol.
  • Alcohol withdrawal and Pabrinex protocols (see the drugs factfile). Sepsis Six for suspected infection.
  • Acute liver failure: jaundice, coagulopathy and encephalopathy in a patient with no pre-existing liver disease (paracetamol, viral, drug, Wilson's). Refer early to a liver unit; King's College criteria guide transplant listing.
The deteriorating patientGeneral acute

Background and pathophysiology

  • Most in-hospital cardiac arrests are preceded by hours of physiological decline (falling SpO₂, rising RR, HR and confusion). Recognise, respond, escalate.
  • NEWS2 scores RR, SpO₂ (Scale 1, or Scale 2 for hypercapnic patients when prescribed by a clinician), air or oxygen, systolic BP, pulse, level of consciousness (ACVPU, any new confusion scores 3) and temperature.
  • Common causes: sepsis, hypovolaemia and bleeding, pneumonia, PE, acute coronary syndrome, arrhythmia, heart failure, opioid or sedative toxicity, hypoglycaemia, stroke, seizure, tension pneumothorax, anaphylaxis.

Diagnosis and clinical signs

NEWS2 response thresholds: 0–4 low risk (minimum 12-hourly obs for 0, 4–6-hourly for 1–4). Single parameter scoring 3 or total 5–6: urgent response, hourly obs, review by a clinician competent in acute illness, consider critical care outreach. Total ≥7: emergency response, continuous monitoring, critical care team, consider transfer.
  • Worrying without a high score: "nurse worried", new confusion, rising lactate, mottled skin, low urine output, ↓GCS, drug overdose.
  • Use SBAR (Situation, Background, Assessment, Recommendation) when escalating.

Investigations

  • Do at the bedside: full set of observations, capillary glucose, ECG, ABG or VBG with lactate, and peripheral cultures.
  • Bloods: FBC, U&E, LFTs, CRP, coagulation, troponin, group and save or crossmatch, cultures, D-dimer if appropriate.
  • Imaging: CXR, bedside ultrasound, CT as directed. Review the drug chart (opioids, antihypertensives, sedatives), fluid balance and the escalation plan.

Treatment: the ABCDE approach

Assess and treat as you go. Call for help at the first sign of trouble. Reassess after every intervention.

  • A: Airway. Look, listen, feel. Chin lift or jaw thrust, suction, oropharyngeal or nasopharyngeal airway. Complete obstruction or failing airway: call anaesthetics and 2222.
  • B: Breathing. RR, SpO₂, work of breathing, trachea, percussion, auscultation. Oxygen 15 L/min via reservoir mask if critically ill (target SpO₂ 94–98%, or 88–92% if at risk of hypercapnia). Treat the cause: bronchodilators (asthma or COPD), needle decompression (tension pneumothorax), diuretics (pulmonary oedema), CPAP or NIV.
  • C: Circulation. HR, BP, capillary refill, JVP, heart sounds, urine output, temperature. Two large-bore cannulae, bloods and lactate, ECG. Fluid bolus (e.g. 500 mL crystalloid over <15 min; smaller and more cautious in heart or renal failure). Stop bleeding. Treat arrhythmias. Consider sepsis, PE, tamponade, MI.
  • D: Disability. AVPU or GCS, pupils, glucose, limb movement and posture. Give glucose for hypoglycaemia, naloxone if opioid toxicity (RR low, pinpoint pupils), and consider seizures, stroke, sepsis and drugs.
  • E: Exposure. Temperature, rash, injuries, calves, abdomen, wounds, lines, catheter, bowel chart, drug chart. Preserve dignity, prevent heat loss.
  • Then: Sepsis Six if suspected, senior and outreach review, record everything (time, obs, actions), and revisit the escalation plan and DNACPR or ReSPECT status.
  • Cardiac arrest (ALS, adult): confirm unresponsive and not breathing normally, call 2222 and start CPR 30:2, attach the defibrillator. Shockable (VF/pVT): shock, CPR for 2 min, adrenaline 1 mg and amiodarone 300 mg after the third shock. Non-shockable (PEA/asystole): adrenaline 1 mg as soon as possible, then every 3–5 min. Look for reversible causes (4Hs and 4Ts: hypoxia, hypovolaemia, hypo-/hyperkalaemia and metabolic, hypo-/hyperthermia; thrombosis, tension pneumothorax, tamponade, toxins).
  • Peri-arrest: unstable bradycardia (atropine 500 mcg IV, repeat up to 3 mg, then pacing options) and unstable tachycardia (synchronised DC cardioversion, amiodarone) are covered in the ALS peri-arrest algorithms.

Emergency protocols and red flags

  • NEWS2 escalation policy and the local critical care outreach team. 2222 cardiac arrest or medical emergency call. Sepsis Six (within 1 hour).
  • ALS (Resuscitation Council UK) algorithm. Anaphylaxis, hyperkalaemia, DKA, status epilepticus, and major haemorrhage protocols (see the relevant factfiles).
  • Treatment escalation and ReSPECT / DNACPR: know the patient's status before an emergency. A DNACPR decision does not mean "do not treat".
  • As a student: if you notice a patient getting worse, stay with them, ask a nurse to fetch help or press the emergency bell, and report your findings using SBAR. You will not be expected to lead, but observing carefully is very useful.
Drugs: reactions, overdose and toxicity, alcohol withdrawalGeneral acute

Background and pathophysiology

  • Adverse drug reactions: Type A (predictable, dose-related: bleeding on warfarin, hypoglycaemia on insulin, AKI on NSAIDs) and Type B (idiosyncratic, immune or non-immune: anaphylaxis, rash, SJS/TEN, DRESS).
  • Overdose and poisoning can be deliberate self-harm, accidental, iatrogenic or recreational. A large proportion of intentional overdoses involve several agents and alcohol. Always consider mental health, capacity and safeguarding.
  • Alcohol withdrawal: chronic alcohol enhances GABA inhibition and suppresses glutamate; sudden cessation leads to CNS hyperexcitability and autonomic overactivity. Wernicke's encephalopathy (thiamine deficiency) often coexists.

Diagnosis and clinical signs

Important drug reactions

  • SJS/TEN: fever, painful mucosal erosions, target lesions, skin detachment with Nikolsky sign, 1–3 weeks after starting a drug (allopurinol, carbamazepine, lamotrigine, phenytoin, sulfonamides, NSAIDs, nevirapine).
  • DRESS: fever, widespread rash, facial oedema, eosinophilia, hepatitis or nephritis, lymphadenopathy, 2–8 weeks after the drug.
  • Serotonin syndrome: agitation, clonus, hyperreflexia, tremor, hyperthermia, diarrhoea (SSRIs, MAOIs, tramadol, linezolid, ondansetron). Neuroleptic malignant syndrome: rigidity, hyperthermia, autonomic instability, raised CK (antipsychotics, stopping dopaminergics).
  • Others: drug-induced liver injury, acute interstitial nephritis, lithium toxicity (tremor, ataxia, vomiting, AKI), digoxin toxicity (nausea, yellow vision, arrhythmias), warfarin or DOAC bleeding, hypoglycaemia (sulfonylureas, insulin), C. difficile.

Overdose

  • Paracetamol: asymptomatic at first (or nausea and vomiting), then RUQ pain, jaundice, hypoglycaemia, coagulopathy and encephalopathy at 24–72 h.
  • Salicylate: tinnitus, sweating, vomiting, hyperventilation, hyperthermia, confusion. Mixed respiratory alkalosis and metabolic acidosis.
  • Opioids: reduced consciousness, pinpoint pupils, slow RR. Benzodiazepines: drowsiness, respiratory depression with alcohol. Tricyclics: anticholinergic effects (dry, flushed, dilated pupils, urinary retention), wide QRS, seizures, arrhythmias. β-blockers, calcium-channel blockers: bradycardia, hypotension, hypoglycaemia. Carbon monoxide: headache, confusion, cherry-red skin (late).
  • Methanol or ethylene glycol: high anion gap acidosis and raised osmolar gap. Iron: vomiting, haematemesis, GI bleeding.

Alcohol withdrawal

  • 6–12 h: tremor, sweating, anxiety, nausea, tachycardia. 12–24 h: hallucinations. 12–48 h: withdrawal seizures. 48–96 h: delirium tremens (confusion, agitation, hallucinations, fever, autonomic instability), with substantial mortality.
  • Wernicke's encephalopathy: confusion, ophthalmoplegia or nystagmus, ataxia (the full triad is uncommon). Untreated can lead to Korsakoff syndrome. Low threshold to treat.

Investigations

  • Overdose: ABCDE, capillary glucose, ECG (QRS, QTc), VBG or ABG, U&E, LFTs, INR, FBC, CK, lactate, alcohol level, pregnancy test. Paracetamol level (at ≥4 h after a single acute ingestion) and salicylate level in all deliberate overdoses with uncertain history, as symptoms may be absent. Get collateral history and tablet counts.
  • Consult TOXBASE (or the National Poisons Information Service, 0344 892 0111) for every poisoning.
  • Paracetamol overdose: INR, ALT, creatinine, bicarbonate and lactate at presentation, repeated over 24–72 h.
  • Alcohol withdrawal: CIWA-Ar score, U&E, Mg²⁺, phosphate, glucose, LFTs, FBC, INR, ECG. Drug reactions: FBC with eosinophils, LFTs, U&E, CK, skin biopsy where needed.

Treatment

Drug reactions

  • Stop the culprit drug and record the reaction on the notes, drug chart and Yellow Card. SJS/TEN: urgent dermatology or burns unit, fluids, wound care, SCORTEN score. DRESS: stop the drug and give systemic steroids on specialist advice. Serotonin syndrome: stop agents, benzodiazepines, active cooling, cyproheptadine. NMS: stop the drug, cool, fluids, consider dantrolene or bromocriptine.

Overdose (general)

  • Resuscitate, monitor, treat specific toxidromes. Activated charcoal 50 g within 1 h of a significant ingestion if the airway is protected (not for iron, lithium, alcohols, acids and alkalis). Psychiatric liaison assessment when medically fit; assess capacity and risk.
  • Paracetamol: single acute ingestion, plot the 4-h level against the treatment line (100 mg/L at 4 h). Treat with IV acetylcysteine (bag 1: 150 mg/kg over 1 h; bag 2: 50 mg/kg over 4 h; bag 3: 100 mg/kg over 16 h). Staggered or unknown-time overdose: treat if ≥75 mg/kg in 24 h, or if the ALT is raised or paracetamol is detectable. Presenting at ≥8 h: start acetylcysteine without waiting for the level. Contact the liver unit if INR rising, pH <7.3, creatinine >300 or encephalopathy.
  • Opioids: naloxone 400 mcg IV (titrated up to 2 mg, or IM), repeat or infusion because it wears off faster than most opioids.
  • Salicylate: IV sodium bicarbonate (urinary alkalinisation), haemodialysis for severe toxicity, avoid intubating without expert help (acidosis).
  • Tricyclic with QRS >100 ms or arrhythmia: IV sodium bicarbonate 50 mL 8.4%. Benzodiazepines for seizures. Avoid flumazenil.
  • β-blocker or CCB: IV calcium, glucagon (β-blocker), atropine, high-dose insulin with glucose, vasopressors. Carbon monoxide: 100% oxygen. Methanol or ethylene glycol: fomepizole (or ethanol) and dialysis. Digoxin toxicity: digoxin-specific antibodies.

Alcohol withdrawal

  • Symptom-triggered or fixed-dose benzodiazepine regimen, usually chlordiazepoxide (e.g. 10–30 mg QDS depending on severity, reducing over 5–7 days per local protocol). Reduce doses or use lorazepam in severe liver disease or frail patients.
  • Thiamine: IV Pabrinex (high-potency vitamin B complex) for patients at high risk or with suspected Wernicke's: 2 pairs of ampoules three times daily for 3–5 days for suspected or established Wernicke's, 1 pair daily for prophylaxis in high-risk patients. Give before or with any IV glucose. Oral thiamine afterwards.
  • Correct K⁺, Mg²⁺, phosphate, treat hypoglycaemia, fluids. Withdrawal seizures: IV lorazepam. Delirium tremens: escalate benzodiazepines, ICU if needed. Antipsychotics only as an adjunct once benzodiazepines are on board.
  • Relapse prevention: alcohol liaison team, acamprosate, naltrexone, disulfiram, and psychosocial support. Watch for refeeding syndrome.

Emergency protocols and red flags

  • TOXBASE / NPIS for every overdose. Paracetamol overdose guideline (MHRA and local). ABCDE, NEWS2, Sepsis Six if infection.
  • NICE CG100 (alcohol-use disorders: acute withdrawal), local Pabrinex and CIWA-Ar protocols.
  • Mental Capacity Act, Mental Health Act and safeguarding: assess capacity and risk before letting a patient leave. Don't let a patient leave mid-course of acetylcysteine without senior review.
  • Report reactions to the MHRA Yellow Card scheme. Severe cutaneous reactions and DRESS mean stopping the drug and asking for senior and dermatology help immediately.
Gastrointestinal (GI) bleedingGastro and liver

Background and pathophysiology

  • Upper GI bleed (proximal to the ligament of Treitz): peptic ulcer (H. pylori, NSAIDs, aspirin), oesophageal or gastric varices, Mallory–Weiss tear, erosive oesophagitis or gastritis, malignancy, angiodysplasia, Dieulafoy lesion.
  • Lower GI bleed: diverticular disease, angiodysplasia, haemorrhoids, colitis (inflammatory, ischaemic, infective), colorectal cancer or polyps, post-polypectomy. A brisk upper bleed can present as fresh PR blood.
  • Risk factors: NSAIDs, aspirin, anticoagulants and antiplatelets, SSRIs, alcohol, liver disease, previous bleeding, H. pylori, critical illness.
  • Severe bleeding leads to hypovolaemia, tissue hypoperfusion and organ failure. Older patients tolerate bleeding poorly and may not mount tachycardia (β-blockers).

Diagnosis and clinical signs

  • Haematemesis, coffee-ground vomit, melaena (black, tarry, foul-smelling), haematochezia. Ask about NSAIDs, anticoagulants, alcohol, liver disease, previous ulcer, vomiting before haematemesis (Mallory–Weiss), weight loss and dysphagia (cancer).
  • Shock: tachycardia, hypotension, postural drop, pallor, cool peripheries, low urine output, confusion. Examine for signs of chronic liver disease, epigastric tenderness, and always do a digital rectal examination.
  • Clues: raised urea with normal creatinine suggests an upper source. Haemoglobin may be normal early because of haemoconcentration.

Investigations

  • Resuscitate first. FBC (repeat), U&E (urea:creatinine), LFTs, clotting and INR, group and save or crossmatch, VBG with lactate, glucose, ECG and troponin if indicated.
  • Glasgow-Blatchford score (GBS) (before endoscopy): a score of 0–1 identifies patients who can be managed as outpatients. Rockall (after endoscopy) predicts rebleeding and mortality.
  • Endoscopy (OGD) within 24 h of admission after resuscitation, and within 12 h (or as soon as safely possible) if suspected variceal bleeding or unstable. Take biopsies or test for H. pylori.
  • Lower GI: colonoscopy when stable. CT angiography for ongoing bleeding with instability, then interventional radiology (embolisation).

Treatment

  • ABCDE. Two large-bore IV cannulae, IV crystalloid to restore perfusion, oxygen if hypoxic, catheter and fluid balance, nil by mouth, high-dependency care if unstable.
  • Transfusion (restrictive strategy): transfuse if Hb <70 g/L, aiming for 70–90 g/L (higher threshold, about 80 g/L, if cardiovascular disease). In exsanguinating haemorrhage, activate the major haemorrhage protocol (red cells, plasma, platelets in balanced ratios). Tranexamic acid is not recommended for GI bleeding (HALT-IT trial: no benefit, more VTE).
  • Anticoagulants and antiplatelets: withhold. Warfarin: vitamin K plus PCC if life-threatening. DOACs: reversal agents if life-threatening bleeding. Restart aspirin (secondary prevention) once haemostasis achieved, as advised by the team.
  • Non-variceal upper GI bleeding: endoscopic therapy (adrenaline injection combined with thermal coagulation or clips) for high-risk stigmata. Then high-dose PPI infusion (80 mg bolus then 8 mg/h for 72 h) after endoscopic therapy. PPI before endoscopy is not recommended by NICE. Eradicate H. pylori. Stop NSAIDs. Surgery or IR if endoscopic haemostasis fails.
  • Variceal bleeding: terlipressin 2 mg IV (then 1–2 mg 4–6 hourly, for up to 5 days) started as soon as suspected, prophylactic antibiotics (e.g. ceftriaxone 1 g daily, for up to 7 days), endoscopic band ligation, restrictive transfusion. Rescue: balloon tamponade (Sengstaken–Blakemore tube) and early TIPSS for uncontrolled or early rebleeding. Then non-selective β-blocker (carvedilol or propranolol) and repeat banding.
  • Lower GI bleeding: most stop spontaneously. Investigate; treat the cause (endoscopic clip, embolisation, surgery). VTE prophylaxis with mechanical devices initially.

Emergency protocols and red flags

  • Major haemorrhage protocol (call 2222 or blood bank; trauma and obstetrics have separate ones). Local upper GI bleeding pathway (NICE CG141 / BSG guidance). Variceal bleeding care bundle.
  • ALS for arrest. Escalate if SBP <100, HR >100, ongoing haematemesis, rising lactate or transfusion requirement, and inform the endoscopist and on-call surgeon and IR early.
  • Patients in alcohol withdrawal also need thiamine and monitoring (see the drugs factfile). Sepsis can accompany variceal bleeding and cirrhosis.
Heart failure (acute and chronic)Cardiovascular

Background and pathophysiology

  • Clinical syndrome of breathlessness, fatigue and fluid retention caused by structural or functional cardiac abnormality, with raised natriuretic peptides or objective evidence of congestion.
  • By ejection fraction: HFrEF (LVEF ≤40%), HFmrEF (41–49%), HFpEF (≥50%).
  • Causes: ischaemic heart disease, hypertension, valve disease, cardiomyopathy (dilated, alcohol, chemotherapy, viral), arrhythmia (AF, tachycardia), congenital, cor pulmonale, high-output states (anaemia, thyrotoxicosis), pericardial disease.
  • Pathophysiology: reduced cardiac output triggers neurohormonal activation (sympathetic nervous system, RAAS, vasopressin) leading to salt and water retention, vasoconstriction and adverse remodelling, which is the basis for treatment.
  • Acute decompensation triggers: missed medication, dietary salt or fluid excess, arrhythmia (AF), ischaemia or MI, infection, anaemia, renal failure, drugs (NSAIDs, negative inotropes, thiazolidinediones), uncontrolled hypertension, PE, valve failure.

Diagnosis and clinical signs

  • Symptoms: exertional dyspnoea, orthopnoea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea, ankle swelling, fatigue, weight gain, nocturnal cough.
  • Signs: raised JVP, displaced apex beat, third heart sound (gallop), bibasal crackles, pleural effusions, peripheral or sacral oedema, hepatomegaly, ascites. Cold, clammy peripheries with hypotension suggests cardiogenic shock.
  • Acute pulmonary oedema: severe breathlessness, frothy pink sputum, tachypnoea, hypoxia, widespread crackles.
  • Haemodynamic profiles: "warm and wet" (congested, well-perfused: most common) versus "cold and wet" (congested and hypoperfused: worse prognosis).

Investigations

  • NT-proBNP (NICE NG106: >2000 ng/L, urgent specialist assessment and echo within 2 weeks; 400–2000 ng/L, within 6 weeks). Levels are raised by age, AF, renal failure and lowered by obesity.
  • ECG (AF, LBBB, LVH, ischaemia), CXR (ABCDE: Alveolar oedema, Kerley B lines, Cardiomegaly, Dilated upper lobe vessels, pleural Effusions), echocardiogram (LVEF, valves, RV, pulmonary pressure).
  • Bloods: U&E (baseline for ACEi and diuretics), FBC, ferritin and TSAT (iron deficiency), TFTs, LFTs, HbA1c, lipids, troponin (ACS as a trigger), lactate in shock. ABG if respiratory distress.
  • Further: cardiac MRI, coronary angiography or CT coronary angiography, and ambulatory ECG as directed.

Treatment

Acute heart failure

  • Sit upright, monitor, IV access, oxygen only if SpO₂ <94%. IV loop diuretic: furosemide 40–80 mg IV (higher if on regular diuretic, typically 1–2.5× oral dose). Monitor urine output (± catheter), reassess response and repeat as needed.
  • CPAP or NIV for pulmonary oedema with respiratory distress or failure. IV nitrates for pulmonary oedema with high BP (avoid if SBP <100). Opioids are no longer recommended routinely.
  • Cardiogenic shock: urgent senior and critical care review, echo, inotropes or vasopressors, mechanical support, treat the cause (revascularise, arrhythmia).
  • Treat the trigger (ACS, AF, infection, anaemia). Daily weights, fluid balance, U&E and K⁺, and stop or hold harmful drugs (NSAIDs, non-dihydropyridine CCBs, thiazolidinediones).

Chronic HFrEF: the four pillars

  • ACE inhibitor (or ARB, or ARNI sacubitril–valsartan), β-blocker (bisoprolol, carvedilol, nebivolol), MRA (spironolactone, eplerenone), and SGLT2 inhibitor (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin). Start low, go slow, monitor U&E and K⁺ after starting or changing doses.
  • Loop diuretics for symptom control. Add ivabradine (sinus rhythm, HR ≥75), hydralazine plus nitrate (particularly in patients of African or Caribbean family origin), digoxin, IV iron if iron deficient.
  • Devices: ICD (primary prevention where LVEF ≤35% despite therapy), CRT (LBBB with wide QRS). Cardiac rehabilitation, vaccinations, salt and fluid advice, self-monitoring of weight, specialist heart failure nurse follow-up within 2 weeks of discharge.
  • HFpEF: diuretics for congestion, SGLT2 inhibitor, treat hypertension, AF and obesity. Advanced HF: palliative care input.

Emergency protocols and red flags

  • Acute pulmonary oedema pathway and NICE NG106 (chronic HF) with ESC guidance. ALS for arrest, ACS pathway if ischaemic trigger.
  • Escalate if SBP <90, persisting hypoxia or acidosis on NIV, rising lactate, or falling urine output.
  • AKI is common when diuresing or starting ACEi (see the AKI factfile). Avoid IV fluids unless the patient is clearly hypovolaemic.
HypertensionCardiovascular

Background and pathophysiology

  • Primary (essential) hypertension (~90%): multifactorial (genetics, salt intake, obesity, age, alcohol, inactivity). Secondary (~10%): renal parenchymal and renovascular disease, primary hyperaldosteronism (Conn's), phaeochromocytoma, Cushing's, coarctation, thyroid disease, obstructive sleep apnoea, pregnancy (pre-eclampsia), drugs (NSAIDs, steroids, oral contraceptive, decongestants, ciclosporin, alcohol, cocaine).
  • Pathophysiology: raised peripheral resistance (RAAS, sympathetic overactivity, arterial stiffness) and sodium retention.
  • Target organ damage: LVH and heart failure, coronary disease, stroke, retinopathy, CKD, aortic dissection, peripheral arterial disease.
  • Hypertensive emergency: severe hypertension (often ≥180/120) with acute target organ damage: encephalopathy, intracerebral haemorrhage or ischaemic stroke, acute coronary syndrome, acute pulmonary oedema, aortic dissection, AKI, eclampsia, papilloedema or retinal haemorrhages (malignant or accelerated hypertension). Hypertensive urgency: severe hypertension without organ damage.

Diagnosis and clinical signs

NICE NG136 thresholds: clinic BP ≥140/90 then confirm with ABPM (daytime average) or home BP (HBPM). Stage 1: clinic ≥140/90 and ABPM/HBPM ≥135/85. Stage 2: clinic ≥160/100 and ABPM/HBPM ≥150/95. Stage 3 (severe): clinic SBP ≥180 or DBP ≥120.
  • Usually asymptomatic. Emergency: headache, visual disturbance, confusion, seizures, chest pain, breathlessness, focal neurology.
  • Examination: BP in both arms, fundoscopy (hypertensive retinopathy, papilloedema), LVH, heart failure signs, radio-femoral delay (coarctation), renal bruits, abdominal masses (PKD), cushingoid features.
  • Look for causes in young patients (<40), resistant hypertension, severe or sudden onset, hypokalaemia, or unusual features.
  • On the ward: BP is often raised due to pain, anxiety, missed medication, fluid overload or acute illness. Repeat with the right cuff size, at rest, and treat the cause. Avoid aggressive BP lowering for asymptomatic raised readings.

Investigations

  • Assess target organ damage and cardiovascular risk: urine ACR and dipstick (haematuria), U&E and eGFR, HbA1c, lipids, ECG, fundoscopy.
  • Emergency: ECG, troponin, CXR (oedema, widened mediastinum), CT head (neurology), CT aorta if dissection suspected, U&E, urinalysis, blood film, pregnancy test.
  • Secondary causes: renin and aldosterone (ARR), urinary or plasma metanephrines, renal artery imaging, cortisol tests, thyroid tests, sleep study.

Treatment

  • Hypertensive emergency: admit to a monitored area (HDU or ICU). IV labetalol (or GTN for pulmonary oedema or ACS, nicardipine, sodium nitroprusside in critical care). Aim to reduce mean arterial pressure by no more than about 25% in the first hour, then to ~160/100 over 2–6 hours, and to normal over 24–48 h. Precipitous falls risk stroke and ischaemia.
  • Specific exceptions: aortic dissection: SBP <120 rapidly with IV β-blocker (labetalol, esmolol) ± vasodilator. ICH: lower SBP to about 130–140 (see stroke factfile). Acute ischaemic stroke: generally don't lower unless thrombolysing or >220/120. Pre-eclampsia/eclampsia: labetalol, nifedipine, hydralazine plus magnesium sulfate. Phaeochromocytoma: α-blockade first.
  • Hypertensive urgency without organ damage: gradual oral treatment over days, review adherence and cause.
  • Chronic treatment (NICE NG136): lifestyle (salt, weight, alcohol, exercise). Step 1: age <55 (or type 2 diabetes at any age) and not of Black African or Caribbean family origin: ACE inhibitor or ARB; age ≥55, or Black African or Caribbean family origin: calcium-channel blocker. Step 2: ACEi/ARB + CCB (ARB preferred to ACEi in people of Black African or Caribbean family origin), or ACEi/ARB + thiazide-like diuretic if a CCB is unsuitable. Step 3: ACEi/ARB + CCB + thiazide-like diuretic (indapamide). Step 4 (resistant): add spironolactone 25 mg (if K⁺ ≤4.5), or α- or β-blocker.
  • Targets: clinic <140/90 (age <80) or <150/90 (age ≥80); home or ABPM <135/85 (<145/85 if ≥80). Statin for high CV risk.
  • Monitor U&E after starting ACEi/ARB or diuretics.

Emergency protocols and red flags

  • Hypertensive emergency management (local guidance), aortic dissection pathway (CT aortogram, cardiothoracic and vascular referral), stroke BP thresholds, pre-eclampsia pathway with obstetrics.
  • Severe hypertension with headache, visual loss, chest pain or neurology means urgent senior review. NICE NG136.
Infective endocarditisInfection

Background and pathophysiology

  • Infection of the endocardium, usually heart valves (or prosthetic material and device leads). Endothelial damage leads to a sterile platelet-fibrin thrombus that becomes colonised during bacteraemia, forming vegetations that destroy valves and embolise.
  • Organisms: Staphylococcus aureus (acute, aggressive, IVDU, healthcare-associated, prosthetic valves), viridans streptococci (subacute, native valve, dental source), enterococci, Streptococcus gallolyticus (bovis) (associated with colorectal neoplasia), coagulase-negative staphylococci (prosthetic valves), HACEK group, and culture-negative causes (Coxiella, Bartonella, Brucella, fungi).
  • Risk factors: previous endocarditis, prosthetic valves or repairs, congenital heart disease, rheumatic and degenerative valve disease, intravenous drug use, indwelling lines, haemodialysis, cardiac devices, poor dentition, immunosuppression.
  • Immune phenomena result from circulating immune complexes; embolic phenomena from vegetation fragments.

Diagnosis and clinical signs

  • Fever (most common), malaise, weight loss, night sweats, back pain, new or changing murmur (regurgitant), heart failure, and any embolic event.
  • Vascular and embolic: splinter haemorrhages, petechiae, conjunctival haemorrhage, Janeway lesions (painless macules on palms and soles), stroke or TIA, splenic infarcts, renal and limb emboli, mycotic aneurysm.
  • Immunological: Osler's nodes (painful nodules on finger and toe pads), Roth spots (retinal haemorrhages with pale centre), glomerulonephritis (haematuria, proteinuria, AKI), arthritis.
  • Other: splenomegaly, clubbing (late). Prolonged PR interval or new heart block suggests aortic root abscess.
  • Modified Duke criteria (2023 Duke–ISCVID update): major: positive blood cultures (typical organisms or persistent bacteraemia) and evidence of endocardial involvement (echo vegetation, abscess, new valvular regurgitation, or other imaging). Minor: predisposition, fever ≥38 °C, vascular phenomena, immunological phenomena, microbiological evidence not meeting major criteria. Definite: 2 major, or 1 major + 3 minor, or 5 minor.

Investigations

  • At least three sets of blood cultures from separate venepuncture sites, taken before antibiotics where the patient is stable (in a septic or unstable patient, take two or three sets quickly and start treatment).
  • Echocardiography: transthoracic first, then transoesophageal if TTE is negative but suspicion is high, in prosthetic valves, or to look for complications (abscess, perforation).
  • FBC (anaemia, leucocytosis), CRP, ESR, U&E, LFTs, urinalysis (microscopic haematuria), ECG (serial for PR interval), CXR, complement and rheumatoid factor (immune activation).
  • Serology or PCR for Coxiella, Bartonella, Brucella in culture-negative disease. CT, MRI or PET-CT for embolic disease, abscesses and prosthetic valve involvement. Dental assessment and OPG. Screen for HIV and hepatitis.

Treatment

  • Antibiotics: prolonged IV treatment, typically 4–6 weeks (guided by microbiology and the endocarditis team). Empirical therapy depends on local policy and clinical picture: for a stable, subacute native valve illness, cultures are taken first and therapy typically includes a β-lactam (amoxicillin) ± gentamicin; for acute, severe, healthcare-associated or suspected staphylococcal disease, cover with flucloxacillin or vancomycin with gentamicin; prosthetic valve: vancomycin, gentamicin and rifampicin. Follow the local microbiology and cardiology guideline.
  • Endocarditis team (MDT): cardiology, cardiac surgery, microbiology or infectious diseases, and others. Consider OPAT (outpatient IV antibiotics) after stabilisation.
  • Indications for surgery: heart failure from valve dysfunction, uncontrolled infection (abscess, persistent bacteraemia despite therapy, fungal or highly resistant organisms), recurrent emboli with large vegetations (>10 mm), and prosthetic valve dehiscence.
  • Complications to watch for: heart failure, conduction abnormalities, embolic stroke, septic emboli, splenic abscess, mycotic aneurysm, glomerulonephritis and AKI.
  • Anticoagulation is generally avoided unless there's a separate indication (risk of intracranial haemorrhage from mycotic aneurysm or septic emboli).
  • Prevention: NICE does not recommend routine antibiotic prophylaxis for dental procedures (CG64), but advises good oral hygiene and prompt treatment of infection. Address risk factors in IVDU (addiction services, harm reduction).

Emergency protocols and red flags

  • Sepsis Six if septic. Heart failure and stroke pathways for complications. Endocarditis team referral for every confirmed case.
  • New murmur with fever, or any fever in a person with a prosthetic valve or IVDU, means blood cultures and echo.
  • Emergency surgical review: acute severe regurgitation with pulmonary oedema, new heart block (root abscess), or major embolic events.
  • Stroke in a febrile patient with endocarditis: do not thrombolyse (seek stroke and cardiology advice).
Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)Gastro and liver

Background and pathophysiology

  • Chronic relapsing immune-mediated inflammation of the gut in genetically susceptible individuals, triggered by environmental and microbiome factors.
  • Ulcerative colitis (UC): mucosal inflammation, always starting in the rectum and extending continuously proximally. Smoking is protective (non-smokers and ex-smokers are at higher risk).
  • Crohn's disease: transmural, patchy ("skip") inflammation anywhere from mouth to anus (commonly terminal ileum), with granulomas, strictures, fistulae, abscesses and perianal disease. Smoking worsens Crohn's.
  • Extra-intestinal manifestations: peripheral and axial arthritis, erythema nodosum, pyoderma gangrenosum, uveitis or episcleritis, primary sclerosing cholangitis (UC), gallstones and renal stones (Crohn's), osteoporosis, anaemia, and increased VTE risk.
  • Complications: toxic megacolon, perforation, massive haemorrhage, strictures and obstruction, fistulae, malnutrition, and colorectal cancer.

Diagnosis and clinical signs

  • UC: bloody diarrhoea, urgency, tenesmus, nocturnal stools, crampy abdominal pain. Crohn's: abdominal pain (often RIF), non-bloody diarrhoea, weight loss, fever, mouth ulcers, perianal fistulae or abscess, obstructive symptoms.
  • Acute severe UC (Truelove and Witts): ≥6 bloody stools per day plus at least one of: HR >90, temperature >37.8 °C, Hb <105 g/L, ESR >30 mm/h (or CRP >30 mg/L).
  • Signs: pallor, tachycardia, fever, abdominal tenderness and distension, aphthous ulcers, clubbing, perianal disease, extra-intestinal signs. Peritonism, absent bowel sounds and marked distension suggest toxic megacolon or perforation.
  • Differentials: infective colitis (C. difficile, Campylobacter, Salmonella, CMV), ischaemic colitis, diverticular disease, colorectal cancer, coeliac disease, microscopic colitis, medication-induced (NSAIDs, checkpoint inhibitors).

Investigations

  • Stool: culture and C. difficile toxin in every flare, faecal calprotectin (raised in active inflammation).
  • Bloods: FBC, CRP, U&E and K⁺, albumin, LFTs, ferritin, B12, folate, vitamin D, blood cultures if febrile.
  • AXR: colon >5.5–6 cm suggests toxic megacolon, mucosal islands and thumbprinting. Erect CXR or CT for perforation.
  • Flexible sigmoidoscopy (unprepared) with biopsies in severe UC. Colonoscopy with ileal intubation and biopsies when stable. Small bowel imaging (MR enterography, ultrasound, capsule) and MRI pelvis for perianal disease in Crohn's. Screen for HBV, HCV, HIV, TB (IGRA), VZV and CMV before immunosuppression.

Treatment

  • Acute severe UC (medical emergency): admit, IV hydrocortisone 100 mg QDS (or methylprednisolone 60 mg daily), IV fluids, correct K⁺ and Mg²⁺, LMWH VTE prophylaxis (even with bleeding), nutritional support, stool chart, daily bloods and abdominal examination. Avoid NSAIDs, opioids, anticholinergics and antidiarrhoeals (loperamide) (risk of toxic megacolon). Early joint gastroenterology and colorectal surgical review.
  • Day 3 review: if stools >8/day, or CRP >45 mg/L with 3–8 stools/day (Oxford criteria), the risk of colectomy is high. Start rescue therapy (infliximab or ciclosporin, or other biologic or JAK inhibitor as per local policy) or plan surgery. Urgent colectomy for toxic megacolon, perforation, massive haemorrhage or failed medical therapy.

Maintenance: mesalazine, thiopurines (azathioprine), biologics (infliximab, adalimumab, vedolizumab, ustekinumab, mirikizumab), and JAK inhibitors (tofacitinib, upadacitinib).

Ischaemic heart disease (stable angina)Cardiovascular

Background and pathophysiology

  • Coronary artery disease (CAD) with atherosclerotic plaque narrows the epicardial vessels so that myocardial oxygen supply cannot meet demand on exertion or stress, causing stable angina (chronic coronary syndrome). Acute plaque rupture leads to ACS (see the ACS factfile).
  • Other mechanisms: coronary vasospasm (Prinzmetal), microvascular dysfunction, and severe aortic stenosis or anaemia (demand). Risk factors: age, male sex, smoking, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, family history, CKD, obesity, inactivity.
  • Consequences: ischaemic cardiomyopathy and heart failure, arrhythmia, and MI.

Diagnosis and clinical signs

Typical angina requires all three: (1) constricting discomfort in the chest, neck, jaw, shoulder or arm; (2) precipitated by physical exertion; (3) relieved by rest or GTN within about 5 minutes. Atypical: two of three. Non-anginal: one or none.
  • Examination is often normal. Look for risk factors (xanthelasma, hypertension, diabetes), anaemia, valve disease (aortic stenosis), heart failure, peripheral vascular disease.
  • Red flags: angina at rest, crescendo angina, new-onset within the last month, or prolonged pain mean unstable angina/ACS, which needs urgent assessment.

Investigations

  • Resting ECG, FBC, U&E, lipids, HbA1c, TFTs, and echo if murmur or suspected LV dysfunction.
  • CT coronary angiography (CTCA) is first-line for typical or atypical angina (NICE). If inconclusive: functional imaging (stress echo, myocardial perfusion scan, stress MRI). Invasive coronary angiography for high pretest probability or for revascularisation planning. Exercise ECG is not recommended for diagnosis in NICE guidance.
  • Assess risk: calcium score, ejection fraction, ischaemic burden.

Treatment

  • GTN spray for acute relief (teach the patient how to use it and what to do if pain persists beyond 5–10 minutes: call 999).
  • Anti-anginal therapy: first-line β-blocker or rate-limiting calcium-channel blocker (verapamil or diltiazem). If inadequate, use the other class (if combining with a β-blocker, use a dihydropyridine such as amlodipine). Second-line and add-ons: long-acting nitrates, ivabradine, nicorandil, ranolazine.
  • Secondary prevention: aspirin 75 mg (or clopidogrel), high-intensity statin (atorvastatin 80 mg, with ezetimibe as needed), ACE inhibitor or ARB (diabetes, hypertension, HF, CKD), BP and diabetes control, smoking cessation, weight loss, exercise, cardiac rehabilitation.
  • Revascularisation: PCI or CABG for symptoms despite optimal medical therapy, and CABG for left main or multivessel disease (especially with diabetes or poor LV function). Decision by the heart team.

Emergency protocols and red flags

  • Rest pain or unstable pattern: treat as ACS (ECG, troponin, ACS pathway). NICE CG126 (stable angina), CG95 (chest pain of recent onset).
  • Do not give nitrates with recent PDE-5 inhibitors (sildenafil). Caution with β-blockers and rate-limiting CCBs together. Driving (DVLA) advice for angina.
Pleural disease: pneumothorax and pleural effusionRespiratory

Background and pathophysiology

Pneumothorax

  • Air in the pleural space causing lung collapse. Primary spontaneous: no underlying lung disease (tall, thin young men, smoking; ruptured subpleural blebs). Secondary: COPD, asthma, cystic fibrosis, TB, Pneumocystis pneumonia, ILD, lung cancer. Traumatic and iatrogenic (central lines, biopsy, ventilation).
  • Tension pneumothorax: a one-way valve effect raises intrapleural pressure, shifts the mediastinum, and reduces venous return, leading to obstructive shock and arrest. It is a clinical diagnosis.

Pleural effusion

  • Fluid in the pleural space. Transudates (protein <25 g/L): heart failure (most common), cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome, hypoalbuminaemia, hypothyroidism, peritoneal dialysis. Exudates (>35 g/L): pneumonia (parapneumonic effusion and empyema), malignancy, PE, TB, rheumatoid arthritis, SLE, pancreatitis, post-MI (Dressler's), asbestos-related disease. Haemothorax, chylothorax.
  • Light's criteria (for protein 25–35 g/L): exudate if pleural:serum protein >0.5, or pleural:serum LDH >0.6, or pleural LDH >⅔ the upper limit of normal serum LDH.

Diagnosis and clinical signs

  • Pneumothorax: sudden pleuritic chest pain and breathlessness. Reduced chest expansion, hyperresonant percussion, reduced or absent breath sounds. Tension: severe respiratory distress, tachycardia, hypotension, distended neck veins, cyanosis, tracheal deviation away (a late sign).
  • Effusion: breathlessness, dry cough, pleuritic pain. Dull ("stony dull") percussion, reduced breath sounds and vocal resonance, bronchial breathing at the upper edge, trachea away from a large effusion.
  • Empyema / parapneumonic: fever, sepsis, persistent pleuritic pain and raised inflammatory markers despite antibiotics. Malignant effusion: weight loss, known cancer, asbestos exposure (mesothelioma).

Investigations

  • Erect PA CXR: pneumothorax shows a lung edge with no peripheral markings. BTS size: "large" if ≥2 cm rim of air (at the hilum level). Effusion: blunted costophrenic angle (200 mL or more), meniscus, or white-out with mediastinal shift away.
  • Thoracic ultrasound: use for all pleural procedures to guide site of aspiration or drain insertion, and to look for septations.
  • CT chest for complex effusions, suspected malignancy or underlying lung disease.
  • Pleural fluid (send with serum protein, LDH and glucose): appearance, pH (use a blood gas syringe), protein, LDH, glucose, cell count with differential, cytology, Gram stain and culture (including AFB if TB is possible), amylase, ADA. pH <7.2, glucose <3.4 mmol/L, pus, or positive culture indicate pleural infection needing drainage.
  • Look for the cause: echo, BNP, LFTs, albumin, urine protein, CT, thoracoscopy.

Treatment

  • Tension pneumothorax: do not wait for a CXR. High-flow oxygen, immediate needle decompression with a large-bore cannula (2nd intercostal space mid-clavicular line, or 4th–5th intercostal space anterior axillary line per local training) or finger thoracostomy, then insert a chest drain. Call for help.
  • Primary spontaneous pneumothorax (BTS): if <2 cm and minimally symptomatic, observe (conservative management, possibly outpatient with clear advice). If >2 cm or breathless: needle aspiration (16–18G cannula, 2nd ICS mid-clavicular line, up to 2.5 L) or small-bore chest drain.
  • Secondary pneumothorax: admit. If <1 cm, oxygen and observation; 1–2 cm aspiration if minimally symptomatic; >2 cm or breathless: chest drain. Refer to respiratory. Persistent air leak (>5–7 days) or recurrence: thoracic surgery (VATS with pleurodesis).
  • Advice: stop smoking. No flying until resolved (typically 1–2 weeks after), no scuba diving unless definitive surgical treatment has been done.
  • Effusion: treat the cause (diuretics in HF, antibiotics for pneumonia). Therapeutic aspiration for symptomatic relief (stop if cough, chest pain, or after 1.5 L). Malignant effusion: drainage, talc pleurodesis or indwelling pleural catheter. Pleural infection: IV antibiotics, chest drain, intrapleural tPA and DNase, and surgical referral (VATS) if failing.
  • Chest drain safety: insert under ultrasound (for effusions) into the "safe triangle" (5th intercostal space, mid- to anterior axillary line, bordered by the lateral edge of pectoralis major and latissimus dorsi), entering above the rib. Never clamp a bubbling drain. Keep the bottle below chest level. Check for swinging and bubbling. If the drain falls out or is disconnected, cover the site and call for help. Monitor for re-expansion pulmonary oedema after large-volume drainage.

Emergency protocols and red flags

  • Tension pneumothorax is one of the reversible causes in ALS. BTS pleural disease guideline (2023), safe chest drain insertion policy (only trained, supervised clinicians, ultrasound-guided for effusions).
  • Deterioration with a chest drain (blocked, kinked, surgical emphysema, haemodynamic instability): urgent senior review, CXR, check drain. Ventilated or NIV patients with pneumothorax need urgent drainage.
  • Sepsis with effusion: think empyema (see the sepsis factfile).
PyelonephritisRenal and urinary

Background and pathophysiology

  • Infection of the renal pelvis and parenchyma, usually by ascending bacteria from the bladder (or haematogenous spread). E. coli (~80%), Klebsiella, Proteus (urease-splitting, associated with struvite stones), Enterococcus, Pseudomonas.
  • Risk factors: female sex, pregnancy, diabetes, urinary tract obstruction (stones, BPH, tumour), catheters and instrumentation, vesicoureteric reflux, immunosuppression, older age, previous UTI.
  • Complications: sepsis, renal or perinephric abscess, emphysematous pyelonephritis (gas-forming, diabetics), pyonephrosis (infected obstructed system, a urological emergency), AKI, papillary necrosis, and chronic scarring.

Diagnosis and clinical signs

  • Fever and rigors, loin or flank pain, nausea and vomiting, malaise, with or without dysuria, frequency and urgency. Older or frail patients may present with confusion, falls or sepsis without urinary symptoms.
  • Signs: fever, tachycardia, hypotension (sepsis), renal angle tenderness, suprapubic tenderness. Examine for a palpable kidney or bladder and prostatic enlargement.
  • Differentials: renal colic, appendicitis, diverticulitis, pelvic inflammatory disease, basal pneumonia, ectopic pregnancy, testicular pathology, aortic pathology.

Investigations

  • Urine dipstick (nitrites, leucocytes, blood) and MSU culture before antibiotics. Blood cultures if febrile or unwell. Pregnancy test in women of childbearing age.
  • FBC, CRP, U&E and creatinine, glucose, lactate, and clotting if septic.
  • Imaging: urgent renal tract ultrasound or non-contrast CT KUB if obstruction or stone is suspected, if there's sepsis or failure to improve at 48–72 h, or in men and pregnancy. Contrast CT for abscess or emphysematous pyelonephritis.

Treatment

  • Sepsis screening and Sepsis Six if unwell. Fluids, analgesia (avoid NSAIDs in AKI), antiemetics, and antipyretics.
  • Antibiotics (NICE NG111; use local policy and culture results, review at 48 h): oral options include cefalexin (7–10 days), co-amoxiclav (7–10 days, only if culture shows susceptibility), trimethoprim (14 days, only if susceptible) or ciprofloxacin (7 days, with MHRA safety cautions). IV options: co-amoxiclav, cefuroxime, ceftriaxone, ciprofloxacin, gentamicin or amikacin (severe sepsis, ESBL risk; watch renal function and levels).
  • Pregnancy: admit, IV cefalexin or cefuroxime. Avoid trimethoprim in the first trimester and nitrofurantoin near term.
  • Obstructed infected kidney: urgent decompression by percutaneous nephrostomy or retrograde ureteric stent, plus antibiotics. Do not delay.
  • Manage catheters (replace if in situ for >7 days), and address risk factors (stones, diabetes control). Follow up recurrent infections.

Emergency protocols and red flags

  • Sepsis Six and NEWS2 escalation. Urological emergency: infected obstructed system means urgent urology and interventional radiology.
  • NICE NG111 (pyelonephritis) and local antimicrobial guidelines. AKI care bundle if creatinine is raised.
  • Persisting fever after 48–72 h of appropriate antibiotics: image for obstruction or abscess.
Sepsis and infection: meningitis, malaria, pneumonia (and endocarditis)Infection

Background and pathophysiology

  • Sepsis is life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection (Sepsis-3: SOFA score rise ≥2). Septic shock: vasopressors needed to maintain MAP ≥65 mmHg with lactate >2 mmol/L despite adequate fluid resuscitation. Pathogen products trigger cytokine release, vasodilation, capillary leak, coagulopathy and mitochondrial dysfunction, so tissues are under-perfused.
  • Common sources: lung, urinary tract, abdomen, skin and soft tissue, CNS, bone and joint, lines, endocarditis. High-risk hosts: extremes of age, immunosuppression, cancer and chemotherapy (neutropenic sepsis), asplenia, diabetes, cirrhosis, pregnancy, indwelling devices, IVDU.
  • Meningitis: inflammation of the meninges. Bacterial (S. pneumoniae, N. meningitidis, H. influenzae, Listeria in age >60, pregnancy, and immunosuppression), viral (enteroviruses, HSV, VZV), TB and cryptococcal (HIV). Viral encephalitis, mostly HSV, is a separate emergency.
  • Malaria: Plasmodium spp. transmitted by Anopheles mosquitoes. P. falciparum (most severe; usually presents within 3 months of travel, often within 4 weeks; sequesters in microvasculature), P. vivax and P. ovale (dormant hypnozoites, relapse), P. malariae, P. knowlesi. Haemolysis and microvascular obstruction cause anaemia, organ dysfunction and cerebral malaria.
  • Pneumonia: infection of the lung parenchyma. CAP: S. pneumoniae most common, H. influenzae, Mycoplasma, Legionella, S. aureus (post-influenza), viruses (influenza, SARS-CoV-2, RSV). HAP (>48 h in hospital): Gram-negatives and S. aureus. Aspiration pneumonia in dysphagia and reduced consciousness. Pneumocystis in immunosuppression and HIV.
  • Infective endocarditis is covered in its own factfile. Neutropenic sepsis is in the cancer factfile.

Diagnosis and clinical signs

Sepsis

  • Fever or hypothermia, tachycardia, tachypnoea, hypotension, altered mental state, mottled skin, prolonged capillary refill, oliguria, jaundice, bleeding. Older patients often have atypical or subtle presentations.
  • NICE NG51 (2024) uses NEWS2 for adults with suspected infection in hospital: NEWS2 ≥7 = high risk (immediate senior review, antibiotics within 1 hour); 5–6 = moderate-to-high risk (senior review within 1 hour, antibiotics within 3 hours if sepsis remains likely). qSOFA is not recommended as a screening tool.

Meningitis

  • Fever, severe headache, neck stiffness, photophobia, vomiting, reduced GCS, seizures. Non-blanching petechial or purpuric rash suggests meningococcal septicaemia (press a glass on the rash). Kernig's and Brudzinski's signs are insensitive.
  • Encephalitis: confusion, behaviour change, seizures, focal signs. Red flags for herpes simplex encephalitis: temporal lobe features, fever.

Malaria

  • Fever (cyclical only in a minority), rigors, headache, myalgia, vomiting, diarrhoea, jaundice, splenomegaly. Any fever after travel to a malaria-endemic area is malaria until proven otherwise.
  • Severe falciparum: impaired consciousness or seizures (cerebral malaria), respiratory distress, shock, AKI, hypoglycaemia, severe anaemia, acidosis, jaundice, bleeding or DIC, hyperparasitaemia (>10%; >2% is high-risk in a non-immune patient).

Pneumonia

  • Cough, purulent sputum, fever, pleuritic pain, breathlessness, confusion (in older adults). Signs: tachypnoea, focal crackles, bronchial breathing, dullness, increased vocal resonance, hypoxia.
  • CURB-65: Confusion (new), Urea >7 mmol/L, RR ≥30, BP (SBP <90 or DBP ≤60), age ≥65. Score 0–1 low risk (home), 2 consider hospital admission, ≥3 high risk (consider ICU review). Legionella clues: hyponatraemia, diarrhoea, deranged LFTs, recent travel or spa.

Investigations

  • Sepsis: full observations, lactate, blood cultures before antibiotics (do not delay treatment beyond 45 minutes to obtain), FBC, CRP (± procalcitonin), U&E, LFTs, clotting, glucose, VBG, urinalysis and MSU, CXR, and imaging or sampling from the suspected source. Identify source control needs.
  • Meningitis: blood cultures, FBC, CRP, U&E, glucose, lactate, clotting, blood PCR for meningococcus and pneumococcus, throat swab. CT head before LP only if: GCS ≤12, focal neurology, papilloedema, new seizure within 30 minutes, immunocompromise, or abnormal behaviour or bleeding risk. LP: opening pressure, cell count, protein, glucose (with paired plasma glucose), Gram stain, culture, PCR (HSV, VZV, enterovirus). Bacterial: cloudy, neutrophils, high protein, low glucose. Viral: lymphocytes, normal glucose, mildly raised protein. TB: lymphocytes, very high protein, low glucose. Never delay antibiotics for CT or LP.
  • Malaria: thick and thin blood films (repeat at 12–24 h intervals if initially negative, three sets in total) plus a rapid diagnostic test. FBC (thrombocytopenia, anaemia), U&E, LFTs, glucose, lactate, clotting, blood cultures, urinalysis, CXR, G6PD status (before primaquine or tafenoquine), parasite count.
  • Pneumonia: CXR, SpO₂ and ABG, FBC, CRP, U&E, LFTs, blood cultures (moderate to high severity), sputum culture, urinary Legionella and pneumococcal antigens (severe disease), respiratory viral PCR (COVID-19, influenza), HIV test if indicated, pleural fluid sampling if effusion.

Treatment

Sepsis Six (within 1 hour): (1) give oxygen to keep SpO₂ 94–98% (88–92% if COPD), (2) take blood cultures ± other cultures, (3) give IV broad-spectrum antibiotics, (4) give IV fluids (500 mL crystalloid bolus over <15 min, reassess; 30 mL/kg for hypotension or lactate ≥4), (5) check lactate, (6) monitor urine output (hourly).
  • Sepsis: senior review, source control (drain abscess, relieve obstruction, remove infected lines), vasopressors (noradrenaline) and critical care if MAP <65 after fluids or lactate >4, hydrocortisone in refractory shock. Adjust antibiotics to cultures and de-escalate at 48–72 h. VTE prophylaxis.
  • Meningitis (UK Joint Specialist Societies guidance): ceftriaxone 2 g IV every 12 h immediately (or cefotaxime), plus amoxicillin 2 g IV 4-hourly if age >60 or immunocompromised (Listeria). Dexamethasone 10 mg IV QDS for 4 days, started with or just before the first antibiotic dose (stop it if an alternative diagnosis is confirmed; follow local policy and microbiology advice). Add aciclovir 10 mg/kg IV TDS if encephalitis is suspected. Pre-hospital: benzylpenicillin IM/IV for suspected meningococcal disease. Critical care for reduced GCS or shock. Notify UKHSA, and chemoprophylaxis for close contacts (e.g. ciprofloxacin single dose). Droplet precautions for 24 h after starting effective antibiotics.
  • Malaria: severe falciparum: IV artesunate 2.4 mg/kg at 0, 12 and 24 h then daily in a HDU or ICU setting, monitoring glucose, then oral ACT to complete once improving. Uncomplicated falciparum: artemisinin combination therapy (artemether–lumefantrine at 0, 8, 24, 36, 48 and 60 h with fatty food, or dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine), or atovaquone–proguanil. Non-falciparum: chloroquine or ACT, then primaquine or tafenoquine for P. vivax and P. ovale (after G6PD testing). Notifiable disease. Advise on prevention (ABCD: Awareness, Bite prevention, Chemoprophylaxis, prompt Diagnosis).
  • Pneumonia (NICE NG138, BTS): low severity: amoxicillin 500 mg TDS for 5 days (or doxycycline, clarithromycin if allergic). Moderate: amoxicillin plus clarithromycin (or doxycycline), 5 days. High severity: IV co-amoxiclav plus clarithromycin, 7–10 days, escalate per local policy for HAP and aspiration. Controlled oxygen, IV fluids, analgesia, VTE prophylaxis, physiotherapy. Escalate to ICU for persistent hypoxia, shock or ventilatory failure. Complications: parapneumonic effusion or empyema, lung abscess, ARDS, AF. Follow-up CXR at about 6 weeks for smokers over 50 or anyone with persisting symptoms.

Emergency protocols and red flags

  • Sepsis Six and the local sepsis pathway (NICE NG51), NEWS2 escalation, and the ABCDE approach. Neutropenic sepsis (antibiotics within 1 h).
  • Meningitis pathway (antibiotics before imaging or LP if delayed), UKHSA notification for meningococcal disease and malaria, meningococcal contacts prophylaxis, infection control (droplet or airborne precautions).
  • Malaria: urgent senior and infectious diseases review for suspected severe disease. Pneumonia: BTS CAP guideline, Legionella notification, COVID-19 or influenza isolation.
  • Other infections to know: C. difficile (see IBD), necrotising fasciitis (pain out of proportion, rapidly spreading, urgent surgical debridement), cellulitis, and infection in the immunosuppressed and returning travellers.
Sickle cell disease and sickle cell crisisHaem and oncology

Background and pathophysiology

  • Autosomal recessive haemoglobinopathy: β-globin point mutation (Glu6Val) produces haemoglobin S (HbS). Genotypes: HbSS (sickle cell anaemia), HbSC, HbS/β-thalassaemia. Sickle trait (HbAS) is usually asymptomatic.
  • Pathophysiology: deoxygenated HbS polymerises, red cells sickle, become rigid and adhere to endothelium, causing vaso-occlusion (pain and organ infarction), chronic haemolysis (anaemia, jaundice, gallstones, endothelial dysfunction), and inflammation.
  • Triggers: infection, dehydration, cold, hypoxia, acidosis, stress, alcohol, pregnancy, surgery. Functional hyposplenism means high risk of overwhelming infection with encapsulated organisms (pneumococcus, H. influenzae, Salmonella).

Diagnosis and clinical signs

  • Vaso-occlusive (painful) crisis: severe pain in the bones, back, chest or abdomen, often similar to previous episodes. Fever is possible with crisis alone but must be treated as infection until proven otherwise.
  • Acute chest syndrome: new pulmonary infiltrate on CXR with fever, cough, chest pain, dyspnoea or hypoxia. Leading cause of death. May follow a painful crisis or infection.
  • Other emergencies: stroke or TIA, splenic sequestration (children; rapid splenomegaly and falling Hb), aplastic crisis (parvovirus B19; low reticulocytes), haemolytic crisis, sepsis and osteomyelitis, priapism, acute multi-organ failure, renal papillary necrosis and AKI, hepatic sequestration and cholestasis, retinal disease.
  • Chronic features: pallor, jaundice, leg ulcers, avascular necrosis, growth delay, pulmonary hypertension, CKD, gallstones.
  • Patients are often experts on their own condition and have a care plan and baseline Hb (often 60–90 g/L in HbSS). Believe their pain report and check for stigma-related under-treatment.

Investigations

  • FBC (compare with baseline; Hb, WCC, platelets), reticulocytes, blood film (sickle cells, Howell–Jolly bodies), LDH, bilirubin, U&E, LFTs, CRP, group and save (with extended red cell phenotype: inform the transfusion lab that the patient has sickle cell disease).
  • Blood cultures if febrile, urine culture, CXR (all patients with chest symptoms), ABG or SpO₂ monitoring, ECG. CT head urgently if neurological deficit. Ultrasound for splenic or hepatic sequestration, and other tests for suspected pain mimics (e.g. appendicitis, osteomyelitis, DVT).
  • Diagnosis is by haemoglobin electrophoresis or HPLC (screened at birth in the UK).

Treatment

  • Analgesia within 30 minutes of arrival (NICE NG143), reassess every 30 minutes. Offer a strong opioid per the patient's care plan (morphine, diamorphine or fentanyl) plus paracetamol ± NSAID if renal function allows. Use PCA for severe pain. Monitor RR, sedation, SpO₂ (NEWS2). Avoid pethidine. Titrate to pain and function, treat constipation and nausea.
  • Hydration: oral or IV fluids to euvolaemia. Avoid overhydration (pulmonary oedema and acute chest syndrome risk). Oxygen if SpO₂ <95%. Keep warm. Incentive spirometry and mobilisation. VTE prophylaxis.
  • Fever ≥38 °C or unwell: treat as sepsis: cultures, and urgent IV broad-spectrum antibiotics per local policy (e.g. ceftriaxone).
  • Acute chest syndrome: oxygen, analgesia (avoid over-sedation), antibiotics (cephalosporin plus macrolide), careful fluids, early haematology and critical care review. Consider simple or exchange transfusion for hypoxia, progression, or multi-organ involvement.
  • Transfusion: simple transfusion for symptomatic anaemia, aplastic crisis and sequestration (avoid raising Hb above ~100 g/L, as this increases viscosity). Exchange transfusion for stroke, severe acute chest syndrome, multi-organ failure, and some refractory priapism. Always use antigen-matched blood and discuss with haematology.
  • Stroke: urgent CT, exchange transfusion and stroke team. Priapism (>2–4 h): urgent urology and haematology, analgesia and hydration. Splenic sequestration: transfusion and haematology.
  • Long term: hydroxycarbamide (increases HbF), penicillin V prophylaxis and immunisations (pneumococcal, meningococcal, Haemophilus, influenza, hepatitis B), folic acid, regular transfusion for stroke prevention, other disease-modifying agents through specialist services, and bone marrow transplant in selected patients. Specialist haematology follow-up. Pregnancy needs specialist care.

Emergency protocols and red flags

  • NICE NG143 (sickle cell disease: acute painful episodes) and the local sickle cell crisis pathway with individual care plans. Inform on-call haematology early. Sepsis Six and stroke pathways where relevant.
  • Red flags: chest pain or hypoxia (acute chest syndrome), fever, neurological symptoms, fall in Hb, priapism, abdominal pain with distension, and rapidly progressive pain that is different from usual.
  • Inpatient monitoring includes NEWS2, sedation score and respiratory rate. Do not delay analgesia while awaiting tests or "proof".

Background and pathophysiology

  • Lower UTI: cystitis, urethritis, prostatitis, epididymo-orchitis. Upper UTI: pyelonephritis (see its own factfile). Ascending infection from perineal or bowel flora. E. coli in most cases; Staphylococcus saprophyticus (young women), Klebsiella, Proteus, Enterococcus, Pseudomonas (catheters).
  • Risk factors: female anatomy, sexual activity, pregnancy, menopause, catheter, diabetes, BPH and retention, stones, neurogenic bladder, immunosuppression, previous UTI.
  • "Complicated" UTI: male, pregnant, catheterised, structural or functional abnormality, immunocompromised, or systemic illness.
  • Asymptomatic bacteriuria is common in older adults and catheterised patients and is not treated (except in pregnancy and before certain urological procedures).

Diagnosis and clinical signs

  • Lower: dysuria, frequency, urgency, nocturia, suprapubic pain, haematuria, cloudy or offensive urine. Prostatitis: fever, perineal pain, hesitancy, retention. Epididymo-orchitis: unilateral scrotal pain and swelling (exclude torsion urgently in the young).
  • Older adults: do not diagnose UTI from confusion or a positive dipstick alone. Look for new urinary symptoms, fever or systemic signs, and think of other causes (pneumonia, constipation, drugs, dehydration).
  • Catheter-associated UTI: fever, rigors, new delirium, suprapubic or flank pain, malaise.
  • Differentials: STI (chlamydia, gonorrhoea), vaginitis and atrophic vaginitis, PID, interstitial cystitis, renal or bladder stones, bladder cancer, prostatitis.

Investigations

  • Women under 65 (NICE NG109): ≥3 typical symptoms (dysuria, new nocturia, cloudy urine) can be treated without dipstick. If 1–2 symptoms, dipstick: nitrite and leucocytes both positive suggests UTI; negative for both makes UTI unlikely. Dipstick is unreliable in people aged ≥65 and in catheterised patients.
  • Send an MSU culture for: men, pregnant women, age ≥65, visible haematuria, recurrent or treatment-resistant infection, catheters, atypical presentation. Take cultures before antibiotics.
  • FBC, CRP, U&E, lactate, blood cultures if systemically unwell or septic. Pregnancy test. Sexual health screen where relevant.
  • Imaging: ultrasound and bladder scan for men, recurrent UTI, retention, haematuria or upper tract symptoms. Visible haematuria (age ≥45) or persistent non-visible haematuria with infection should trigger urgent cancer pathway referral.

  • Simple lower UTI, non-pregnant women (NICE NG109, 3 days): nitrofurantoin 100 mg modified-release BD (if eGFR ≥45), or trimethoprim 200 mg BD (if low resistance risk), or pivmecillinam 400 mg then 200 mg TDS. Fosfomycin 3 g single dose is an alternative if resistance. Men: nitrofurantoin or trimethoprim for 7 days (consider prostatitis). Pregnancy: nitrofurantoin (avoid at term), cefalexin, for 7 days, guided by culture.
  • Catheter-associated UTI (NG113): treat only if symptomatic; change the catheter if it has been in for >7 days, and send culture. Antibiotic course for 7 days (or shorter if mild).
  • Acute prostatitis: ciprofloxacin or ofloxacin for 14 days (or trimethoprim for 14 days), review at 48 h. Epididymo-orchitis: guided by age and risk (STI: ceftriaxone IM plus doxycycline; enteric organisms: ofloxacin or co-amoxiclav).
  • Advise fluids, paracetamol, and return if there's fever, loin pain, vomiting or worsening (possible upper UTI or sepsis). Manage recurrent UTI: behavioural advice, post-coital or continuous antibiotic prophylaxis, methenamine hippurate, vaginal oestrogen in postmenopausal women, and review for underlying causes.
  • Never treat asymptomatic bacteriuria except in pregnancy or before urological instrumentation.

Emergency protocols and red flags

  • Sepsis Six and NEWS2 if systemically unwell. Urosepsis with obstruction (stone, retention): urgent decompression.
  • NICE NG109 (lower UTI), NG113 (catheter-associated), NG111 (pyelonephritis), and local antimicrobial stewardship policies. Check allergy and renal function before nitrofurantoin or trimethoprim.
  • Testicular torsion is a surgical emergency: acute testicular pain in a young man needs urgent surgical review, not antibiotics.
Venous thromboembolism: DVT and pulmonary embolismHaem and oncology

Background and pathophysiology

  • DVT: clot in deep veins, usually of the leg (proximal: popliteal, femoral, iliac; distal: calf). PE: embolus (usually from a proximal DVT) lodges in pulmonary arteries.
  • Virchow's triad: venous stasis, endothelial injury, hypercoagulability. Risk factors: immobility, recent surgery or trauma, active cancer, pregnancy and postpartum, oestrogen (COCP, HRT), previous VTE, thrombophilia, obesity, older age, long-haul travel, sepsis, nephrotic syndrome, IBD, COVID-19.
  • PE effects: raised pulmonary vascular resistance, V/Q mismatch (hypoxia), right ventricular strain and failure. Massive (high-risk) PE: sustained hypotension (SBP <90 for ≥15 min) or shock, leading to obstructive shock and arrest.
  • Complications: recurrent VTE, post-thrombotic syndrome, chronic thromboembolic pulmonary hypertension.

Diagnosis and clinical signs

  • DVT: unilateral leg swelling (calf circumference >3 cm difference measured 10 cm below tibial tuberosity), pain, warmth, erythema, pitting oedema, dilated superficial veins, tenderness along the deep veins.
  • PE: sudden dyspnoea, pleuritic chest pain, haemoptysis, syncope or pre-syncope, tachycardia, tachypnoea, hypoxia, low-grade fever. Signs of RV strain: raised JVP, loud P2, RV heave. High-risk PE: hypotension, syncope, cardiac arrest.
  • Two-level Wells score for PE (≤4 unlikely, >4 likely): clinical signs of DVT (3), PE is the most likely diagnosis or an alternative is less likely (3), HR >100 (1.5), immobilisation ≥3 days or surgery in the last 4 weeks (1.5), previous PE or DVT (1.5), haemoptysis (1), malignancy (1).
  • Wells score for DVT (≥2 likely): active cancer, paralysis or recent immobilisation, bedridden ≥3 days or surgery within 12 weeks, localised tenderness along deep veins, entire leg swollen, calf swelling >3 cm, pitting oedema, collateral superficial veins, previous DVT (each 1); alternative diagnosis as likely or more likely (−2).
  • Differentials for PE: pneumonia, pneumothorax, ACS, pericarditis, heart failure, aortic dissection, musculoskeletal pain, anxiety. Differentials for DVT: cellulitis, ruptured Baker's cyst, muscle injury, heart failure oedema, lymphoedema.

Investigations

  • Suspected DVT (NICE NG158): two-level Wells. DVT likely: proximal leg ultrasound within 4 h (if delayed, give interim anticoagulation), and D-dimer if ultrasound is negative. DVT unlikely: D-dimer (if positive, ultrasound).
  • Suspected PE: two-level Wells. PE likely: CTPA immediately (interim therapeutic anticoagulation if delay). PE unlikely: D-dimer (age-adjusted for age >50: age × 10 µg/L), CTPA if positive. Alternative imaging: V/Q scan (normal CXR, renal impairment, contrast allergy, pregnancy, young patients).
  • Baseline tests: ECG (sinus tachycardia, S1Q3T3, RBBB, right heart strain T-wave inversion V1–V4, AF), CXR (often normal; wedge infarct, small effusion), ABG or SpO₂ (hypoxia, respiratory alkalosis), FBC, U&E and eGFR (drug choice and dosing), LFTs, clotting, group and save.
  • Risk stratification: troponin, BNP, bedside echo (RV dilatation, McConnell's sign), CT signs of RV strain, and sPESI or PESI score. Hestia criteria for outpatient care.
  • Consider cancer if unprovoked (history, examination, and basic tests only, as directed by NICE), thrombophilia testing only in select cases (specialist advice).

Treatment

  • Anticoagulate as soon as suspected if imaging will be delayed and there is no contraindication.
  • First line (NICE NG158): apixaban (10 mg BD for 7 days, then 5 mg BD) or rivaroxaban (15 mg BD for 21 days, then 20 mg OD). Alternatives: LMWH (weight-based) with dabigatran or edoxaban, or LMWH with warfarin (INR 2–3, LMWH overlap for ≥5 days until INR ≥2 for 24 h). Cancer-associated VTE: apixaban, edoxaban, rivaroxaban or LMWH. Pregnancy: LMWH. Antiphospholipid syndrome: warfarin. Renal impairment (eGFR <15–30) and possible thrombolysis: UFH.
  • Duration: at least 3 months. Provoked by a transient risk factor: stop at 3 months. Unprovoked: review at 3 months and consider extended treatment, balancing bleeding risk. Active cancer: continue while cancer active. Recurrent VTE: indefinite.
  • Massive (high-risk) PE: resuscitate, oxygen, careful fluids, vasopressors as needed, systemic thrombolysis (e.g. alteplase 10 mg bolus then 90 mg over 2 h) after excluding contraindications (active bleeding, recent intracranial haemorrhage or ischaemic stroke <3 months, intracranial structural disease, recent major surgery or trauma), or catheter-directed therapy or surgical embolectomy. Cardiac arrest with suspected PE: alteplase 50 mg bolus and prolonged CPR.
  • IVC filter: only if anticoagulation is absolutely contraindicated or PE recurs despite adequate anticoagulation.
  • Compression stockings are not routinely recommended. Outpatient management for selected low-risk patients (sPESI 0 / Hestia negative). Distal DVT: treat or serial ultrasound surveillance per local policy.
  • Prevention: all admitted patients need a VTE risk assessment within 24 hours. LMWH (e.g. enoxaparin 40 mg SC daily; 20 mg if eGFR <30) unless bleeding risk is high, in which case use mechanical prophylaxis (intermittent pneumatic compression), with regular reassessment.

Emergency protocols and red flags

  • NICE NG158 (VTE diagnosis, management and thrombophilia testing) and the local PE pathway. Massive PE algorithm with critical care and senior involvement. ALS: thrombosis as a reversible cause.
  • Syncope, hypotension, hypoxia, or RV strain on ECG or echo: treat as high-risk and escalate immediately.
  • Bleeding on anticoagulants: stop the drug, and give reversal (e.g. idarucizumab for dabigatran, andexanet or PCC for factor Xa inhibitors, vitamin K and PCC for warfarin) per local guideline. Check renal function and weight before DOAC dosing.
