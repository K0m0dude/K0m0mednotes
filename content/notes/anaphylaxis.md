background: >+
  - Severe, life-threatening systemic hypersensitivity reaction. Usually
  **IgE-mediated**: allergen cross-links IgE on mast cells and basophils,
  releasing histamine, tryptase and leukotrienes. Results in vasodilation,
  capillary leak, bronchospasm and laryngeal oedema.

  - Non-IgE mechanisms (direct mast-cell activation) occur with NSAIDs, opioids,
  IV contrast and some neuromuscular blockers.

  - **Triggers:** food (nuts, shellfish, milk, egg), drugs (β-lactams, NSAIDs,
  neuromuscular blockers, chlorhexidine, contrast), venom (wasp, bee), latex.
  Around 1 in 3 have no identified trigger. Higher risk with asthma,
  mastocytosis, β-blockers or ACEi, and exercise or alcohol as co-factors.

diagnosis: >+
  > **Likely anaphylaxis when all are present:** sudden onset and rapid
  progression, life-threatening airway and/or breathing and/or circulation
  problems, **and** skin or mucosal changes (flushing, urticaria, angioedema).
  Skin changes can be absent in up to 20% and may not appear until after BP
  falls.


  - **A:** hoarse voice, stridor, throat tightness, swollen tongue or lips.
  **B:** wheeze, breathlessness, cyanosis, hypoxia. **C:** tachycardia,
  hypotension, pallor, collapse. **D:** confusion, agitation, sense of doom,
  reduced consciousness.

  - Also GI symptoms (vomiting, cramps, diarrhoea). Exposure to a known trigger
  in the previous minutes to hours.

  - Differentials: vasovagal syncope, panic attack, asthma, hereditary
  angioedema (no urticaria, no response to adrenaline), septic or cardiogenic
  shock, PE.

investigations: >+
  - **Do not delay treatment for tests.** Anaphylaxis is a clinical diagnosis.

  - **Mast cell tryptase:** first sample as soon as possible, second **1–2 h
  after onset** (no later than 4 h), third at ≥24 h (baseline, or at follow-up).
  A raised acute level relative to baseline supports the diagnosis; a normal
  level does not exclude it.

  - Bedside: SpO₂, ABG if severe, ECG.

  - **Referral to an allergy clinic** for every case (identify the trigger,
  provide adrenaline auto-injectors, training and a written action plan).

treatment: >+
  - **Remove the trigger** if possible (stop the infusion), call for help, note
  the time.

  - **IM adrenaline 1 mg/mL (1:1000), anterolateral mid-thigh:** adult and child
  >12 y **500 mcg (0.5 mL)**; 6–12 y 300 mcg (0.3 mL); 6 months–6 y 150 mcg
  (0.15 mL); <6 months 100–150 mcg. **Repeat every 5 minutes** if there's no
  improvement.

  - **Position:** lie flat with legs raised (sit up if breathing is difficult,
  left lateral if pregnant). Do not stand or walk the patient up.

  - **High-flow oxygen** (15 L/min via reservoir mask). **IV fluid bolus**
  500–1000 mL crystalloid for hypotension.

  - Antihistamines and steroids are **not** part of initial resuscitation (RCUK
  2021) and do not treat airway, breathing or circulation problems. They can be
  used later for skin symptoms.

  - **Refractory anaphylaxis** (no response after 2 IM doses): call critical
  care or anaesthetics for an **IV adrenaline infusion** (specialist setting),
  further fluids, and glucagon 1–2 mg IV if on a β-blocker.

  - **Observe** for a minimum of 6–12 h depending on severity (longer after
  severe reactions or if more than one adrenaline dose was needed; biphasic
  reactions can occur). Discharge with **two adrenaline auto-injectors**,
  training, avoidance advice and allergy referral.

protocols: >+
  - **Resuscitation Council UK anaphylaxis algorithm** (ABCDE, adrenaline
  first). Call 2222 if airway compromise, refractory shock or collapse.

  - Cardiac arrest: follow ALS and give IM or IV adrenaline as per algorithm.

  - Report suspected drug reactions to the **MHRA Yellow Card** scheme and
  record the allergy in the notes and on the drug chart.
